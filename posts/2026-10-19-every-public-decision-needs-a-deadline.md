In Los Angeles, New York and San Francisco, a standard 20-unit apartment building takes about 4.6 years to complete. In Raleigh, Orlando and Phoenix it takes roughly two. As much as 43 percent of that gap is not construction at all. It is time spent waiting for a permit.

Delay is no longer just a side effect of how public institutions work. More and more, it is how they ration. A city that will not say no to a new building can simply take longer to say yes. A program with more eligible people than budget can let a long form and a longer wait thin the queue, more quietly than any cut in eligibility. The cost lands on whoever has the least time to spare, and because it is paid in hours rather than dollars, almost nobody counts it.

The figures above come from a recent study by economists at the University of Chicago and Princeton, who examined permit records from 60 American cities. Between 2000 and 2023, the approval time for such a project rose from about 0.6 years to 1.5. In 2000 it had a 71 percent chance of approval within six months; by 2025 the odds had fallen to 44 percent. Across cities, permitting time accounts for about half the variation in rents, and each extra year of it is associated with rents 39 percent higher.

Housing is the clearest case, not the only one. The Office of Management and Budget estimates that the public spent 10.5 billion hours in fiscal 2023 responding to federal forms and information requests, the equivalent of about five million full-time workers. The hassle bites hardest where help matters most: the I.R.S. estimates that one in five workers eligible for the earned-income tax credit do not claim it.

I work in corporate finance, and part of that job is tracking how long cash and approvals sit stuck, because idle time has a price. Governments already accept this principle, but only in one direction. When a federal agency owes a supplier money, the Prompt Payment Act of 1982 starts a clock: an invoice is generally due 30 days after it arrives, and a late payment carries an interest penalty. Europe's Late Payment Directive gives public authorities 30 days to pay a business, 60 at most, before statutory interest accrues. When a government owes a citizen a decision, nothing happens. No clock starts, no interest runs, and the cost of waiting stays with the person who is waiting.

Europe has had a remedy on its books since 2006. Its Services Directive requires that permission to run many kinds of business be decided within a period "fixed and made public in advance." If no answer comes in time, the authorization "shall be deemed to have been granted," unless overriding public-interest reasons justify another rule. Germany's administrative procedure law has a similar "deemed approval" clause, with a default clock of three months that starts once the file is complete. But the German rule applies only where a specific law switches it on, and the European one allows exceptions. The principle is written down; what is missing is the ambition to apply it widely.

"Silence means yes" sounds reckless until you consider the rule we already accept: silence means wait, for as long as the office likes, at the applicant's expense. A clock flips that incentive. An office that must decide within 90 days, or see an application approved by default, will spend its scarce attention on the cases that truly need scrutiny and wave through the routine ones. Not every decision suits default approval; nobody wants a deemed license for a chemical plant. But a loft conversion, a café's outdoor tables, a new shop sign or the renewal of an unchanged business license are another matter. Where default approval is too risky, the clock can trigger something else instead: automatic escalation to a senior official, a refunded fee, a written reason for the hold-up.

The obvious objection is that offices will dodge the clock by rejecting files or declaring them incomplete. Europe's rule shows where the gap lies: its clock runs only once all documentation has been submitted, and an office must merely flag missing papers as quickly as possible. So the completeness check needs a short deadline of its own, after which the file counts as complete. Refusals should come with written reasons and a quick route of appeal; the directive already requires that a refusal be fully reasoned and open to challenge. And publishing refusal and incompleteness rates would show who is gaming the clock.

Clocks work only if the waiting is visible. Publishing median decision times, office by office, the way airlines publish punctuality, would turn delay from private misery into a public figure someone must answer for. And counting the hours as a cost, as the federal government already does for paperwork, would let officials weigh a new requirement against the time it consumes.

Artificial intelligence makes this urgent. Software can read a building application in minutes, check it against the code and flag the parts that need a human planner. The same budget can instead buy an agency phone bot that answers every call and never puts the caller through to a person. An office with a deadline has every reason to use AI to decide faster. An office without one has every reason to use it to keep callers at a distance.

The burden of waiting is not shared equally. A professional with a flexible schedule, or the money to hire help, can absorb a six-month wait or a two-hour phone hold. A shift worker, a caregiver or someone working in a second language often cannot, so rationing by hassle falls hardest on the very people many programs were built to help. Anyone who has moved to a new country, as I did when I came to Germany, learns how much of settling in is discovering which queue to join. A deadline tells the applicant that their time has value, and that the cost of delay belongs to the institution that controls the pace.

The idea is modest. It needs no new agency and little new money. Governments already pay interest when they keep a supplier waiting; they should hold themselves to the same discipline with citizens. Every routine decision should come with a published deadline. When the deadline passes without an answer, the clock should decide.
