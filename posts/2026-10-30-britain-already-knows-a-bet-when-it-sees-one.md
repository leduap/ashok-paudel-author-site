In March a company called Kalshi UK Limited appeared on the register at Companies House. Kalshi is one of the largest American prediction markets, platforms where users buy contracts that pay out if something happens: a team wins, a city reaches a certain temperature, a film tops the box office. Today both Kalshi and its rival Polymarket list the United Kingdom as a restricted jurisdiction in their own terms, so neither lets people here open new trades. A British subsidiary raises an obvious question. If these platforms come, under which rules?

In the United States they call it trading, and Kalshi answers to the regulator of derivatives, not casinos. The figures tell a more familiar story. According to an analysis of the platform's trading data published in August, sport and parlays, those all-or-nothing bundles of bets, make up about 80% of Kalshi's volume. Users aged 18 to 21, who are barred from most American sportsbooks, traded an estimated $5.4bn on it this year. In July, according to published reports, 44 state attorneys general wrote to the federal regulator describing these platforms as "a new form of casino".

Britain has already answered the question America is fighting over in court. In February, the Gambling Commission said that if a prediction market operator launched here, it did not believe the operator could classify itself as a non-gambling product. The core of these platforms, it said, is akin to a betting exchange, a model Britain has long regulated as gambling. The Financial Conduct Authority says the financial versions it has examined are binary options, which it banned for retail customers in 2019; at the time it called them "gambling products dressed up as financial instruments". In September, Europe's securities regulator noted that selling event contracts in the EU generally requires an authorisation that the largest platforms do not currently hold.

Any pressure to soften that position is likely to be polite. It may come as a request for a "proportionate" new category, a sandbox, or a nod to the value of forecasting, each resting on the idea that a contract which looks like a financial instrument on screen deserves to be treated like one in law.

I head a global controlling team, and in my world this question was settled long ago. Accounting records transactions by their economic substance, not merely their legal form; the global accounting standard-setter's framework says so almost word for word. Calling a lease a service contract does not keep it off the balance sheet. Auditors look at what the contract does. Apply that test to a screen that says "buy yes at 62p" on Saturday's match. If the contract pays out when one team wins and loses everything when it does not, it does what a bet does, whatever the button says.

That matters because of what a licensed bet already comes with. In Britain an 18- or 19-year-old can legally bet on football, and nobody is proposing to change that. What protects them is the package attached to the bet. An online operator must verify a customer's age and identity before they can gamble. Since April 2020, no licensed operator may take a credit card for gambling, because gambling on borrowed money deepens harm. Online operators must run checks for signs of financial vulnerability, such as a county court judgment or a bankruptcy, once a customer's deposits minus withdrawals pass £150 in 30 days. They must sign up to GAMSTOP, so that one request can shut a person out of every licensed site at once. And since April 2025 every licensee pays a statutory levy that funds research, prevention and treatment of gambling harm.

None of that depends on what the product is called. All of it would apply to a licensed betting exchange, which is what the Gambling Commission says these platforms most resemble. Britain is also willing to protect young adults by age when the evidence calls for it: since May last year, online slot players aged 18 to 24 have been limited to a £2 stake per spin, against £5 for older adults. That rule covers slots, not sports bets, but it shows the state looking at the customer, not the machine.

Forecasting markets are not worthless. Prices that pool thousands of opinions about the weather, the harvest or the date a bridge reopens can tell us things that polls and experts miss, and some of that activity belongs in properly supervised markets. But the useful part does not need a sportsbook bolted on to pay the bills.

So the principle Britain should carry into any talks is simple. Protection follows the customer, not the label. If a contract pays out on a football score, it is a bet, and it comes with everything a licensed bet comes with: verified age and identity, no credit cards, financial checks, self-exclusion and the levy. If a firm wants to call itself an exchange, it can apply for the licence that betting exchanges here already hold. What it cannot do is buy an exemption with vocabulary.

America is learning, in court, what happens when the label decides. Britain need not repeat the experiment. Any controller could tell the regulators what an auditor would say: look at what the contract does, and account for it accordingly.
