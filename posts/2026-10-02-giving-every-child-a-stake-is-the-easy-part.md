In finance there is a special place for money that nobody can match to an owner. Accountants call it a suspense account. Every company has one, and every controller I know treats it with suspicion, because a balance that cannot be traced to a person or a purpose is not yet an asset. It is a question. I have spent much of my career, first as a consultant and now running global controlling for an aerospace manufacturer, chasing those questions until they had answers.

I have been thinking about suspense accounts since Oct. 1, when the U.S. Treasury [announced](https://home.treasury.gov/news/press-releases/sb0642) that it had automatically created investment accounts, known officially as Trump Accounts, for more than 60 million children. Add the [$1,000 federal deposit](https://www.irs.gov/trumpaccounts) for babies born from 2025 through 2028 and Michael and Susan Dell's [$6.25 billion pledge](https://www.onedell.com/investamerica/) to put $250 into the accounts of 25 million young children, and this is one of the most ambitious attempts any country has made to give a generation a financial stake from birth.

The idea is right. Ownership changes how people see an economy. A teenager who watches even a small portfolio compound learns something no classroom can teach, and young people in many countries worry, with reason, that the doors to housing and wealth are closing on them. An early share of the upside is a constructive answer.

But the debate so far is about deposits: how much, from whom, for which children. Almost none of it is about the moment that decides whether the money does any good, the day an 18-year-old has to find it, prove it is hers and decide what to do with it. The experience of places that tried this earlier is sobering.

Britain gave children born between 2002 and early 2011 a Child Trust Fund seeded with at least £250, and where parents did not open one, the government opened it for them. As of April 2025, according to HM Revenue & Customs, [758,000 matured accounts](https://www.gov.uk/government/news/savings-stash-worth-thousands-waiting-for-758000-young-people) belonging to 18- to 23-year-olds had not been claimed, worth £2,242 each on average. The state created the accounts, funded them, let them grow for 18 years and then lost touch with their owners. It now runs a locator tool and publicity campaigns to reunite young adults with their own money.

India, where I trained as an accountant, has the same problem at larger scale. Bank deposits left untouched for 10 years are [transferred](https://rbi.org.in/Scripts/FAQView.aspx?Id=165) to the Reserve Bank of India's Depositor Education and Awareness Fund. That fund held ₹97,545 crore, about $11 billion, at the end of March 2025, up from ₹78,213 crore a year earlier, according to the central bank's [annual report](https://rbi.org.in/Scripts/AnnualReportPublications.aspx?Id=1442). In 2023 the RBI launched a [portal](https://rbi.org.in/Scripts/BS_PressReleaseDisplay.aspx?prid=56216) simply to help people search for their own money across banks. In the United States, the National Association of Unclaimed Property Administrators [estimates](https://unclaimed.org/what-is-unclaimed-property/) that about one in seven people has unclaimed property sitting with a state.

None of this comes from fraud or bad intentions. It comes from a design that puts the burden of reconciliation on the person least equipped to carry it. People move, change names and phone numbers, lose letters. A parent who opened an account in 2004 may never mention it to a son who turns 18 in 2022. Every institution in this story knew exactly whom the money belonged to on the day it was deposited. Over the years, the link quietly broke.

The American accounts begin with the same weakness. Treasury has automated their creation, which is a real improvement. But a parent must still [claim](https://home.treasury.gov/news/press-releases/sb0642) the account in an app, verifying identity and relationship, before the child can receive the $1,000 federal seed or contributions from relatives and employers. The families most likely to skip that step are the ones the policy most wants to reach.

Anyone who has moved a company's finances onto one system, as I did for teams in seven countries, learns that the hard part is not the software. It is settling, before go-live, who owns each balance and how that ownership will be proven every month, so that nothing drifts into suspense and stays there. Public programs for children need the same discipline, and most of it comes down to choices made at the start, not at 18.

The first choice is to make the system, not the family, responsible for keeping the link alive. An account should be tied to the identifier a person keeps for life, a Social Security number in America or a National Insurance number in Britain, and whoever administers it should be required to check contact details against official records every year. If an account can be created automatically, it can be found automatically.

It also matters that the core money never depends on a form. Israel's [savings plan for every child](https://www.btl.gov.il/English%20Homepage/Benefits/Children/Savings%20plan/Pages/default.aspx), running since January 2017, shows how. The National Insurance Institute deposits a monthly sum for every child who receives child allowance (58 shekels in 2026) and pays the management fees until age 21. Parents can pick a bank or fund and double the deposit, and many do not: in 2023, the institute [reported](https://www.btl.gov.il/English%20Homepage/About/PressReleases/Pages/ReportHisachonLekolYeled2024.aspx), more than a third of newborns' plans were placed in a default fund because their parents had not chosen one. Yet the state's money arrives either way, because nobody has to apply for it.

What remains is the handover, which deserves the care a company gives a product launch rather than a letter in the post. Well before 18, the young person should be able to see the account on her phone, with a plain statement of what it holds and what it has earned. Israel adds a grant of [582 shekels at 21](https://www.btl.gov.il/English%20Homepage/Benefits/Children/Savings%20plan/Pages/SavingsAmount.aspx) for those who leave their savings untouched, a nudge to treat a first stake as capital rather than pocket money. Technology has made the rest cheap. Britain's tax authority already lets young people [keep](https://www.gov.uk/government/news/savings-stash-worth-thousands-waiting-for-758000-young-people) their National Insurance number in a phone's digital wallet; the same wallet could hold a pointer to a first investment account.

Done well, the handover is the most valuable part of the gift. For many 18-year-olds it will be the first time they own a piece of the economy rather than merely work in it or buy from it. A statement showing $1,000 grow over 18 years teaches compounding better than any lecture. A forgotten account teaches nothing.

Countries are about to spend billions of dollars of public and philanthropic money giving children a stake. The deposits will make headlines; the reconciliation will not. It should. In any ledger, money that cannot find its owner is not yet wealth. It is a question no one bothered to answer.
