A Random Walk Down Wall Street was first published in 1973. Its author, Burton G. Malkiel, is an economist at Princeton University, and he has revised the book many times since. The thirteenth edition came out in 2023 to mark fifty years in print. It remains one of the fullest introductions to investing written for a general reader.

## The short answer

A broad, patient explanation of why share prices are hard to predict and why most investors are better served by saving steadily and holding a diversified set of index funds. It covers far more ground than a short index-fund book: the history of bubbles, the main ways professionals try to pick shares, the theory of risk, and a guide to investing at different ages. It is long, the middle chapters are demanding, the practical advice is American, and the theory at its centre is still argued over. If you want to understand the case for index funds and not only accept it, this is the book.

## Who it is for

- Readers who want to understand the reasoning and evidence behind index investing, not only the conclusion.
- Investors who are tempted by stock tips, charts or star fund managers and want to know what the research says.
- Students of finance who want a readable companion to a textbook.
- Anyone curious about new fashions such as cryptocurrencies and meme stocks, and how an economist judges them.

## Who it is not for

- Readers who want a quick answer. A shorter book will get you to the same place.
- Readers outside the United States looking for local guidance on accounts and tax.
- Anyone hoping for a method to beat the market. The book argues against the idea.

## How the book is organised

The paperback of the thirteenth edition is listed at 432 pages. I have not been able to check its chapter list, so the outline here comes from the catalogue record of the ninth edition (2007), and newer editions have added and rearranged material.

That edition runs in four broad movements. It opens with how shares are valued and with the history of speculation: "Firm Foundations and Castles in the Air", "The Madness of Crowds", and chapters on valuation from the 1960s to the 1990s and on the internet bubble. It then tests how professionals pick shares, in chapters on technical and fundamental analysis. Next comes the theory: "A New Walking Shoe: Modern Portfolio Theory", "Reaping Reward by Increasing Risk", "Behavioral Finance" and "Potshots at the Efficient-Market Theory and Why They Miss". It ends with the practical part: "A Fitness Manual for Random Walkers", a primer on what returns to expect from stocks and bonds, "A Life-Cycle Guide to Investing" and "Three Giant Steps Down Wall Street".

## Key ideas

### What "random walk" means

A random walk, in the book's sense, is a price path in which the next step cannot be predicted from the steps before it. If share prices behave this way, nobody can reliably forecast short-term moves, and consistently beating the market average becomes very hard.

Malkiel's best-known demonstration is a classroom experiment. His students built a chart for an imaginary share that started at fifty dollars and moved up or down by half a point each day on the flip of a coin. He then showed the chart to a chartist, a professional who reads price patterns, who saw a pattern and said the share should be bought at once. The pattern had been produced by chance.

"Random" here means unforecastable. It does not mean that prices are senseless, and it does not mean the market goes nowhere: the long-run direction of a growing economy's shares can be upward while the path there is unpredictable.

### Two ways of picking shares, both tested

Technical analysis studies charts of past prices in the belief that patterns repeat. Fundamental analysis studies a company's business, earnings and prospects to estimate what its shares ought to be worth. Malkiel explains each fairly and then sets it against academic studies. He finds significant flaws in both, and concludes that for most investors these methods will do worse than simply holding the market.

### Bubbles are old, and they rhyme

The early chapters are history: crowds carried away by rising prices, from earlier centuries to the internet shares of the late 1990s. The purpose is practical. A reader who has seen the pattern several times is better placed to recognise the next one. The publisher says the fiftieth anniversary edition brings this up to date by critically analysing cryptocurrencies, NFTs (digital collectibles) and meme stocks (shares driven up by online enthusiasm).

### Past winners rarely stay winners

Malkiel makes the same case against choosing actively managed funds by their track record. He cites studies showing that funds which do well in one period often fall behind in the next, a drift back towards average. Since only a few funds beat their benchmark index over the long run, the chance that an ordinary investor picks them in advance is small.

### Risk, and investing by age

The theory chapters explain why spreading money across many holdings reduces risk without necessarily reducing return, and why higher expected returns come with higher risk. The life-cycle chapter turns this into a plan that changes with age, on the principle that the mix of shares and bonds that suits a young saver is not the one that suits someone about to retire.

### The practical conclusion

According to the publisher, the book shows why an individual who saves consistently over time and buys a diversified set of index funds can achieve above-average results. "Above-average" follows from the argument: if most professional investors trail the market after their costs, then matching the market cheaply puts you ahead of most of them. The newest edition also covers tax-aware investing and newer techniques, including factor investing, risk parity and ESG portfolios.

## What works

- **It teaches how to think about markets.** A reader finishes able to weigh a new investment idea, not only knowing one recommended product.
- **It takes the other side seriously.** Stock-picking methods are explained fairly before they are tested, and there is a full chapter answering critics of the theory.
- **It is kept up to date.** Few investing books have been revised as often, by the original author, over fifty years.
- **It gives a plan as well as a theory.** The closing chapters cover what to do at different ages.
- **The writing is clear** for a book by an academic economist.

## What to be aware of

- **It is long.** More than 400 pages, and a reader who only wants the recommendation will find it in the final quarter.
- **The middle is denser than the rest.** The chapters on portfolio theory and risk ask more of the reader than the history or the practical advice.
- **The theory is disputed.** The book is closely tied to the efficient-market hypothesis, the idea that prices already reflect available information. Investors such as Warren Buffett and many behavioural economists reject it, and two finance professors answered Malkiel with a book titled A Non-Random Walk Down Wall Street. Malkiel himself has written that the weight of evidence supports the theory while conceding that the data contain what he called "gremlins". The practical advice does not need markets to be perfectly efficient, only hard to beat after costs.
- **The author has ties to the index-fund industry.** Malkiel was a director of the Vanguard Group for 28 years and is chief investment officer of Wealthfront, an automated investing service. That does not undo the evidence, but a reader should know it.
- **It is written for American investors.** The practical guidance assumes United States accounts and tax rules.
- **Older editions date quickly at the edges.** The core has not changed in decades, but the discussion of current fashions, products and tax belongs to the year of each edition.

## How it compares

[The Little Book of Common Sense Investing](../2026-09-21-review-the-little-book-of-common-sense-investing/) reaches the same conclusion in far fewer pages, arguing mainly from costs. Bogle shows that the average investor must lose to the index; Malkiel also explains why picking the exceptions is so hard. [The Simple Path to Wealth](../2026-09-07-review-the-simple-path-to-wealth/) is the easiest of the three and the lightest on evidence.

[The Intelligent Investor](../2026-10-05-review-the-intelligent-investor/) is the classic of the method Malkiel tests under the name fundamental analysis, and reading both is the best way to hear each side. [The Psychology of Money](../2026-09-14-review-the-psychology-of-money/) covers investor behaviour through stories where Malkiel uses research.

## The verdict

The best single book for a reader who wants to understand why the case for index funds is made, and who is willing to give it the time. Read the history and the practical chapters closely; take the theory chapters slowly or skim them on a first pass. If you want the conclusion without the tour, read Bogle instead.

## FAQ

### Which edition is the latest?

The thirteenth edition, published in 2023 for the book's fiftieth anniversary. The paperback is the one linked on this page.

### What does "random walk" mean?

That the next move in a share price cannot be predicted from its past moves. It is the reason the book doubts that anyone can pick winners consistently.

### Do I need the newest edition?

The core argument has been the same for decades. The newer editions add discussion of recent developments such as cryptocurrencies and meme stocks, and current tax and product detail.

### Does the book say markets are always right?

No. It gives several chapters to bubbles, when prices were badly wrong. Its claim is narrower: that such errors are very hard to profit from reliably.
