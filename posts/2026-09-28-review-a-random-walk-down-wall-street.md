A Random Walk Down Wall Street first appeared in 1973, before index funds for ordinary investors existed. Burton Malkiel, an economist at Princeton, has revised it many times since. It remains one of the fullest introductions to investing written for a general reader.

## What the book covers

The title refers to the idea that short-term share price movements cannot be reliably predicted, so a portfolio chosen at random would do about as well as one chosen by experts. Malkiel uses that idea as the spine of a much wider book.

It has four broad parts:

1. **Bubbles and their history,** from the Dutch tulip mania and the South Sea Bubble to more recent episodes.
2. **How professionals try to beat the market.** He examines technical analysis, which studies price charts, and fundamental analysis, which studies companies, and explains why neither has delivered consistent results.
3. **The academic theories,** including diversification, risk and return, and the findings of behavioural finance.
4. **A practical guide** to building a portfolio at different ages.

## What works

- **It covers the whole field.** Few single books explain bubbles, valuation, academic theory and personal portfolio planning together.
- **It is fair to the other side.** Malkiel takes the claims of chart readers and stock pickers seriously enough to test them.
- **The history is entertaining.** The chapters on past manias are the most enjoyable in the book.
- **It has been kept current.** Later editions address newer topics as they have arisen.

## What to be aware of

- **It is long.** Read cover to cover it is a real commitment. The practical chapters at the end can be read on their own.
- **Some sections are technical.** The chapters on academic theory ask more of the reader than the rest.
- **Editions differ.** Because the book has been revised so often, make sure you are reading a recent one.
- **The practical advice is aimed at readers in the United States.**

## Who should read it

Readers who want to understand why the case for index funds exists, not only what it says. If The Little Book of Common Sense Investing is the short version of the argument, this is the full one.
