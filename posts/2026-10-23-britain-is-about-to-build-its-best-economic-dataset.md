Anyone who has worked in a finance department knows the routine. Figures the business already holds – what it sold, to whom, for how much – are typed again into a government questionnaire, checked by someone senior and sent off. Weeks later, cleaned and weighted, they turn up in a headline about growth. It is an oddly old-fashioned way for a modern economy to learn how it is doing.

Britain is about to get the chance to change that. At last year's budget the government confirmed that from April 2029 every VAT invoice between businesses, and between businesses and the public sector, must be issued electronically in a structured format that machines can read. An implementation roadmap is due with next Wednesday's budget. The case made for it is about tax and efficiency: less fraud, faster payment for small suppliers, and invoicing costs the government says could fall by 60% to 80%. Good reasons all. Yet the most valuable by-product goes almost unmentioned: the government's long consultation response covers compliance, cyber security and late payment, and barely touches national statistics.

That is a big omission. A structured e-invoice records who sold what to whom, when, at what price and on what payment terms. Add those up across every VAT-registered business and you get what no survey can produce: a running picture of how money moves through the economy, by industry and region, built as trade happens rather than pieced together months later.

The need is real. This autumn the statistics regulator formally cancelled the accreditation of figures drawn from the Labour Force Survey, after a long suspension. The Office for National Statistics has rebuilt response rates and is preparing an online-first replacement, but it says itself that running the old and new surveys side by side is straining quality and resources and limiting what else it can improve. Household surveys will always matter, because no invoice tells you who is looking for work. That is exactly why the business side of the statistics should stop competing for the same scarce money.

The ONS has already started down this road. Since 2017 it has used VAT returns from around three-quarters of a million businesses alongside its monthly business survey, easing the burden on smaller firms; by its own reckoning VAT data feeds about 14% of the output measure of GDP. But a VAT return is a quarterly total spread across a handful of boxes. An e-invoice is the transaction itself.

Other countries show where this can lead. Italy made electronic invoicing between businesses mandatory in 2019, routed through its tax agency, and its finance ministry now publishes regular reports on sectoral and regional activity taken straight from the e-invoice database. The EU will require digital reporting of cross-border invoices from 2030. In Germany, where I work, every business has had to be able to receive structured e-invoices since January 2025, and larger firms must start issuing them in January 2027.

My own trade teaches one lesson here: whether the data is any good will be settled now, in dull meetings about standards, not in 2029. When I helped move finance teams in seven countries onto a single system, the decisions that mattered most were about definitions, not software: what counts as a product line, how a customer is identified, which fields are mandatory and which are optional. Get that master data right at the start and reporting comes almost free. Get it wrong and you spend years reconciling numbers that were never comparable. A national e-invoicing standard is master data for an entire economy. If the roadmap leaves product and industry codes optional, lets each software provider run its own dialect and keeps the statisticians out of the room, Britain will build an excellent tax-compliance machine and a poor statistical one. Fixing that later will cost far more than designing it in now.

So the ONS should help write the standard, not inherit it. The legal basis already exists: since 2017 the Digital Economy Act has let the statistics office draw on data held by other public bodies. The government has chosen a decentralised model and put off real-time reporting, and that is fine. Statistics do not need every invoice the moment it is raised. What they need is an aggregated, anonymised feed held behind the same safeguards that already protect VAT and payroll data, so that nobody outside the statistical system can see an individual firm's prices. And businesses whose invoices feed that picture should get something back: shorter survey forms, or none.

The government's own consultation response notes that transaction data could help it support businesses in a future economic emergency. Anyone who remembers how long it took to see which sectors and towns the pandemic was hitting hardest will understand why that matters.

Britain spends heavily asking businesses questions they have already answered in their own ledgers. From 2029 those ledgers will share a common language. Next week's roadmap will decide whether the country's statisticians get to read it.
