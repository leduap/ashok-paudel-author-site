title: AI's Real Payoff Is on the Factory Floor
date: 2026-10-12

summary: Europe's own data show manufacturers are among the slowest adopters of artificial intelligence. That is the opportunity.
---

When Andreessen Horowitz and Atlas Holdings said they would place AI entrepreneurs directly on the factory floors of manufacturing and distribution companies through a new venture, Foundry Management ([WSJ Pro, Oct. 1](https://www.wsj.com/pro/venture-capital/andreessen-horowitz-and-atlas-holdings-want-ai-entrepreneurs-on-the-factory-floor-fb5711dd)), it may have looked like a niche bet. It is one of the most sensible ideas of the artificial-intelligence boom.

Most of the excitement around AI has gathered where it is easiest to see: chatbots, coding assistants, slick demos in the cloud. The factory has been an afterthought, and the numbers confirm it. According to [Eurostat](https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/documents/7870049/23260410/KS-01-26-009-EN-N.pdf), the European Union's statistics office, 20% of EU businesses with 10 or more employees used at least one AI technology in 2025. Among information and communication companies the share was 62.5%. In manufacturing it was 17.3%, below the average for the economy as a whole.

Even among companies that do use AI, the factory gets surprisingly little of it. [Eurostat finds](https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/documents/7870049/23260410/KS-01-26-009-EN-N.pdf) that 34.7% of AI-using firms apply it to marketing and sales and 31.1% to business administration. Only 20.8% use it in production processes. We have pointed our most powerful new tool at the slide deck rather than the machine.

I see this gap from a particular seat. As head of global controlling for a manufacturing business, my job is to translate what happens on the shop floor into numbers a board can act on. Before that I worked at BCG and Deloitte, and I have led finance teams across seven countries, from the U.S. and Canada to Italy, Sweden, the U.K., Germany and China. In every one of those places the same lesson held: Money in manufacturing is made or lost in small, repeated decisions. How a machine is set up. When a part is inspected. How much material sits waiting. That is precisely the territory where AI, applied patiently, can do the most good.

Consider what a production line generates every hour: sensor readings, maintenance logs, quality records, energy data. Factories are drowning in information no human has time to read. A system that spots the early signature of a wearing tool, flags a batch drifting out of tolerance, or sequences jobs to cut idle time isn't science fiction. It is pattern recognition, the thing these tools already do well.

Why, then, the slow uptake? [Eurostat asked](https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/documents/7870049/23260410/KS-01-26-009-EN-N.pdf). Among EU firms that had considered AI but weren't using it, 70.3% cited a lack of relevant expertise, ahead of legal uncertainty and privacy concerns. Only 17.8% said AI simply wasn't useful for their business. That is why the Foundry Management model is promising: It moves scarce talent to where the problem is, instead of waiting for the problem to find its way to Silicon Valley.

Size matters too. [Eurostat reports](https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/documents/7870049/23260410/KS-01-26-009-EN-N.pdf) that 55% of large EU enterprises use AI, against 17% of small ones. Much of the industrial world's strength lies in midsize and family-owned suppliers, the companies that make the components inside cars, aircraft and machinery. If they are left behind, the whole supply chain is held back.

The stakes are real. The OECD's latest [Compendium of Productivity Indicators](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/oecd-compendium-of-productivity-indicators-2026_734a5e68-en.html) reports that labor productivity across its member countries grew 1.2% in 2024, double the 2023 rate. Yet the EU managed only 0.2%, against 2.2% in the U.S., and manufacturing productivity weakened in several European economies. The OECD also notes that the industries where AI use is most prevalent, information and communication and professional services, posted the strongest efficiency gains in 2024. The sectors that embraced AI are pulling ahead. Factories can follow.

Here is where finance leaders must step in. Too many AI projects are approved on enthusiasm and judged on anecdotes. On a factory floor that is unnecessary, because results can be measured to the cent. Did scrap fall? Did cycle time shrink? Did we free cash that was trapped in inventory and work in progress? Did a machine that used to stop without warning keep running? These aren't soft benefits. They show up in the income statement and on the balance sheet within months.

My advice to fellow executives and finance teams is simple. First, pick one line, one machine or one product family, and set a baseline before any AI arrives. Second, put the people who know the process (operators, engineers, quality inspectors) in the same room as the people who build the models; expertise, not software, is the bottleneck. Third, measure the outcome in the units your plant already uses: scrap, yield, downtime, inventory days. Fourth, if it works, scale it with the same discipline you would apply to buying a new machine.

AI won't transform manufacturing through a keynote speech. It will happen one production line at a time, in plants from Ohio to Bavaria, run by teams that treat it as a tool to be measured rather than a trend to be admired. The investors heading to the factory floor have the right instinct. Business leaders should meet them there, with a stopwatch, a scrap report and a willingness to learn.
