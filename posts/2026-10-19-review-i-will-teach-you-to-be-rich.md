The title sounds like a promise from a late-night advert. The book is better than that. I Will Teach You to Be Rich was first published in 2009, and the second edition, reviewed here, came out in May 2019 from Workman. It is a step-by-step programme for getting your accounts, saving and investing set up so they need little attention afterwards.

## The short answer

One of the most practical personal finance books available to an American reader. It tells you what to do, in what order, and gives you words to use when you phone the bank. Its two real contributions are a way of planning spending that is not a budget and a way of automating money so the plan runs without willpower. The investing advice is simple and mainstream. The banking, credit and account detail applies only to the United States, the named products date quickly, the advice assumes a steady pay cheque with something left over, and the jokey, confident voice is a matter of taste.

## Who it is for

- Readers early in their working lives who want a system, not a philosophy.
- People who know they should sort out their money and keep putting it off.
- Anyone who would sooner spend freely on a few things they love than track every coffee.
- Couples or individuals with a regular salary and several accounts that do not yet work together.

## Who it is not for

- Readers outside the United States, unless they are happy to translate every practical step.
- Readers who already have their accounts and investing on autopilot.
- People with low or irregular income, for whom fixed percentages and scheduled transfers fit badly.
- People who dislike a brash tone.

## How the book is organised

The publisher lists 352 pages and describes the book as a six-week programme. I have the chapter list of the first edition from its catalogue record, and the second edition keeps the six-week shape: "Optimize Your Credit Cards", "Beat the Banks", "Get Ready to Invest", "Conscious Spending", "Save While Sleeping" and "The Myth of Financial Expertise", followed by "Investing Isn't Only for Rich People", a chapter on maintaining the system, and "A Rich Life" on larger decisions.

The order is deliberate: clear up credit and bank accounts first, open investment accounts, decide where the money should go, then automate it, and only then choose investments. The publisher says the second edition adds more than 80 pages, including new tools, material on money and psychology, and stories from earlier readers.

To check the two central ideas I have used the author's own current articles on them, which describe the same systems the book teaches and refer back to its chapters. The percentages below are from those articles, and the book's may differ slightly.

## Key ideas

### Conscious spending, not budgeting

Sethi's Conscious Spending Plan divides take-home pay into four buckets and gives each a rough share. Fixed costs such as rent, utilities, insurance and subscriptions should ideally take 50 to 60%. Investments take about 10%. Savings for specific goals and emergencies take 5 to 10%. The rest, 20 to 35%, is what he calls guilt-free spending.

The last bucket is the point. His complaint about ordinary budgets is that they tell you what you cannot do. Under his plan, once the first three buckets are dealt with, the remainder is meant to be spent, with "no guilt" about the daily coffee or concert tickets.

*An illustration with round numbers, not taken from the book:* on take-home pay of 4,000 a month, the plan would suggest about 2,000 to 2,400 for fixed costs, 400 for investments, 200 to 400 for savings and 800 to 1,400 for guilt-free spending.

### Fixed costs are the number to watch

Sethi treats the fixed-cost share as the first figure to look at. In one example on his site, a couple found that 91% of their take-home pay went on fixed costs, and his reading was blunt: at that level there is nothing left to save or invest, whatever happens in the other buckets. He also suggests adding a buffer of about 15% to fixed costs for the expenses people forget.

### Big wins instead of fifty small cuts

He argues that people who try to economise on fifty things end up saving a little on each and giving up. His alternative is to pick the two largest areas of optional spending and cut them by a quarter to a third over six months. It is the opposite of advice to give up small treats.

### Automate the flow

The chapter "Save While Sleeping" turns the plan into machinery. Pay arrives in the current account, which he compares to an email inbox: everything lands there and is then filed. On set days shortly afterwards, automatic transfers move money to retirement accounts and savings, bills are paid automatically, and what remains is the month's spending money.

Two practical details show the level he works at. First, ask each company to move its billing date so that everything falls just after payday. Second, keep a buffer in the current account, for which he suggests $500, in case a transfer goes wrong. His worked example is a saver who, after setup, spends less than two hours a month on her finances.

He also recommends starting tiny if needed: an automatic transfer of $5 proves the system works, and it is easier to raise an amount than to begin.

### Ask, with a script

The book assumes that banks and card companies will often waive a fee or change a term if asked properly, and it supplies the words. The publisher highlights word-for-word scripts for talking your way out of late fees. For many readers the discovery that these things are negotiable is worth the price of the book.

### Simple investing

The chapter titles "The Myth of Financial Expertise" and "Investing Isn't Only for Rich People" state the position: do not count on experts to pick winners, and do not assume investing is only for the wealthy. His articles point to an employer's retirement plan first, especially where the employer matches contributions, and then to index funds and target-date funds, which adjust their mix as a chosen retirement year approaches.

## What works

- **It gets people to act.** Each week of the programme has its own tasks, in an order that makes sense.
- **Automation is the right emphasis.** A system that runs by itself beats good intentions.
- **It does not moralise about small pleasures.** The focus is on the few large decisions that matter most.
- **The scripts are practical.** Many readers do not know they can ask.
- **It is specific.** Days of the month, percentages and buffer amounts are given, not left to the reader.

## What to be aware of

- **It is written for the United States.** Credit scores, the retirement accounts it names, employer matching and the products recommended are all American. A reader elsewhere keeps the method and loses most of the detail.
- **Named products date.** Specific banks, cards, apps and offers change after publication.
- **It assumes a steady surplus.** Percentages of take-home pay and transfers timed to payday suit a salaried reader. Someone with irregular income or with fixed costs far above 60% is told what the problem is, but the route out is harder than the tone suggests.
- **The investing advice is brief and ordinary.** It is sound as far as it goes, and the index-fund books cover the reasoning much better.
- **The tone divides readers.** It is informal and sure of itself, and the author also sells paid programmes through his website.
- **"Rich" is defined loosely.** The book is about living well on your own terms, not about a number, and the title promises more than any book can.

## How it compares

[Your Money or Your Life](../2026-10-12-review-your-money-or-your-life/) is the opposite approach to spending: record every amount and question each category. Sethi asks for a plan in four buckets and attention to two big items. Robin's method goes deeper; Sethi's is the one more people will keep up.

[The Psychology of Money](../2026-09-14-review-the-psychology-of-money/) explains why people behave badly with money and gives no instructions; this book is nearly all instructions. For the thinking behind the investing chapters, [The Little Book of Common Sense Investing](../2026-09-21-review-the-little-book-of-common-sense-investing/) and [The Simple Path to Wealth](../2026-09-07-review-the-simple-path-to-wealth/) do the job properly.

On editions: the 2019 edition is the one to buy. The first edition dates from 2009, and its product recommendations are older still.

## The verdict

A strong first book for an American reader in their twenties or thirties who wants things set up properly and then left alone. Elsewhere, read it for the two central ideas, the four-bucket plan and the automatic flow, and find the local equivalents yourself.

## FAQ

### What is new in the second edition?

According to the publisher, more than 80 new pages, including new tools, material on money and psychology, and stories from readers of the first edition.

### Does the six-week programme really take six weeks?

The book sets out one block of tasks per week. How long each takes depends on how many accounts you need to open or change.

### Is it useful outside the United States?

The ideas of automating your money and spending deliberately apply anywhere. The account types, credit system and products do not.

### What is a Conscious Spending Plan?

The author's alternative to a budget: take-home pay split into fixed costs, investments, savings and guilt-free spending, with a rough percentage for each.
