Money to Burn is William D. Cohan's account of Leon Black and the investment firm he co-founded, Apollo Global Management. Portfolio published it on 8 September 2026. It entered the New York Times hardcover nonfiction list dated 27 September 2026 at number 11, and the combined print and e-book list for the same week at number 9.

## The short answer

This is a business biography on a large scale: one man, one firm, and through them the shift on Wall Street from banks to private investment firms. Its value for a finance reader is the inside view of how Apollo was built and how it makes money. Its weaknesses, on which several reviewers agree, are length and a habit of handing the story to the people being interviewed. It deals with serious allegations and with Black's financial ties to Jeffrey Epstein, and it does so largely by setting out competing accounts. Expect a demanding read that informs more than it judges.

## Who it is for

- People who work in or around private equity, credit or insurance and want the history of one of the largest firms in the field.
- Readers who already know the outline of the 1980s junk bond era and want to see what its people did next.
- Students of corporate finance who learn well from deals described by those who did them.
- Readers of Cohan's earlier Wall Street histories who like his interview-led style.

## Who it is not for

- General readers looking for a brisk story. Publishers Weekly expects casual readers to find it heavy going.
- Anyone who wants a critical study of what private equity does to the companies it buys and the people who work in them.
- Readers who want a clear verdict on the allegations involving Black. The book reports claims and denials; it does not settle them.
- People with no patience for finance vocabulary.

## How the book is organised

The publisher lists 688 pages for the hardcover and an audiobook of more than 22 hours, read by the author. No table of contents was available on the publisher's page, so this review does not describe chapters. From the publisher's description and the reviews, the shape is roughly this: Black's family background and his father's career; his early years at the investment bank Drexel Burnham Lambert alongside Michael Milken; the founding of Apollo after Drexel failed; the firm's deals and growth, including its stock market listing; and Black's resignation in March 2021 and what followed. The introduction, quoted by one reviewer, presents Black as a reluctant billionaire. The Bloomberg reviewer, as excerpted by the review aggregator Book Marks, says some of the most useful analysis arrives only in the last eight pages.

This review is written from the publisher's details and the published reviews listed under Sources.

## Key ideas

### Apollo grew out of a collapse

Drexel Burnham Lambert was the firm most associated with "junk bonds": high-interest debt issued by companies with weaker credit, often used to pay for takeovers. When Drexel failed, Black and colleagues started Apollo in 1990. According to the American Prospect's review, the new firm specialised in distressed debt, meaning the loans and bonds of companies in trouble, and in leveraged buyouts, where a company is bought mostly with borrowed money that the company itself must then repay. The publisher's phrase for Apollo's style is contrarian: buying what others were avoiding.

### From buyouts to private credit and insurance

The book's wider subject is a change in who lends money. Private equity firms buy whole companies. Private credit means lending directly to companies, outside the banking system and the public bond market. Publishers Weekly explains the piece that connects the two at Apollo: Athene, an insurance business that sells retirement annuities. An annuity is a contract in which a saver hands over money now in return for payments later. Those premiums give the firm a large pool of money to lend. Publishers Weekly notes that many observers compare the arrangement to a bank that sits outside banking regulation, and it rates the book's explanation of these strategies as clear.

### How the money is made

The American Prospect summarises the traditional private equity fee arrangement, known as "two and twenty": the manager takes two percent of the fund each year and twenty percent of the profits.

*An illustration with round numbers, not taken from the book:* a fund holds 100 million. The yearly management fee at 2% is 2 million, whatever happens. If the fund's investments then gain 30 million, the manager's 20% share of that profit is 6 million. The manager receives 8 million in total and the investors keep 24 million of the gain, having paid the 2 million fee as well.

The same review says Apollo listed its own shares in 2011, which turned its founders into billionaires, and that the firm now manages more than a trillion dollars.

### Access is the method

Cohan interviewed Black at length, along with Marc Rowan, who now leads the firm, co-founder Josh Harris and others. The Sunday Times reviewer, as excerpted by Book Marks, notes that Black also gave him documents and suggests he took part in order to repair his reputation. That access is the book's selling point and its problem. The New York Times reviewer calls it a feat of reporting and still objects that the author lets his subjects carry too much of the telling. A reader should treat long quotations from interested parties as their version, not as established fact.

### The fall

Black resigned from Apollo in March 2021. The publisher's description puts his departure down to his own errors of judgment, and names his personal and financial dealings with Jeffrey Epstein among them. Publishers Weekly puts the amount Black paid Epstein for tax advice and other services at 158 million dollars. The book also covers allegations made against Black by several women and his own counter-claims. Those matters are contested, and this review does not attempt to weigh them.

## What works

- **It documents a real shift in finance.** The move of lending and deal-making from banks to private firms affects pensions and insurance policies held by ordinary savers, and a detailed case study is useful.
- **The financial explanation is praised even by critics.** Publishers Weekly, which found the book overlong, still singles out the analysis of Apollo and Athene.
- **Primary material.** Long interviews with the principals are a record that later writers will draw on, whatever one thinks of how they are used here.
- **The author knows the trade.** Cohan is a former investment banker, so the deals are described by someone who understands their mechanics.

## What to be aware of

- **It is very long, and reviewers say it feels it.** Bloomberg's reviewer and Publishers Weekly both complain of extended, repetitive interview excerpts.
- **Access can soften judgment.** The American Prospect argues that the author too often relays what insiders say without testing it. That is one reviewer's opinion, but the New York Times makes a milder version of the same point.
- **The costs of private equity get little space.** The American Prospect faults the book for saying little about workers, patients and towns affected by buyouts, or about attempts to regulate the industry. A reader wanting that side needs another book.
- **It involves disputed claims about living people.** The book presents conflicting accounts. Nothing in it, or in this review, should be read as a finding.
- **It is not a guide to investing.** It describes how a firm earned fees and returns; it gives no reason to think a reader can or should copy any of it.
- **Jargon.** Even a sympathetic trade review warns that the quoted finance talk is hard to follow.

## How it compares

Cohan has written several histories of Wall Street firms; the publisher names House of Cards, The Last Tycoons and Power Failure. Readers who liked those know what to expect. For the opposite emphasis, the Observer's reviewer points to Gretchen Morgenson's These Are the Plunderers, which concentrates on the damage done to companies bought by private equity.

On this site, [1929](../2026-09-11-review-1929/) is a similar exercise in telling financial history through powerful men, and has drawn a similar complaint that analysis gives way to personality. [Buy Then Build](../2026-11-02-review-buy-then-build/) covers buying a company with borrowed money at the scale of one small business, which makes the basic logic of a buyout easier to grasp. [Financial Intelligence](../2026-10-26-review-financial-intelligence/) explains the debt and cash flow terms that this book assumes.

## The verdict

Money to Burn is a thick, detailed source on how one firm helped move a large part of finance out of the banks. It is worth the effort for readers with a professional or serious amateur interest in that change. It is less successful as a story and should be read alongside a more critical account of the industry.

## FAQ

### Is this a book about Jeffrey Epstein?

No. Epstein appears because Black's payments to him led to Black's resignation, but the subject is Black's career and Apollo.

### Do I need to understand private equity first?

Some background helps. A trade review rates the financial analysis as clear, but reviewers also say that long stretches of quoted conversation are hard to follow.

### Did Leon Black cooperate with the author?

Yes. Reviewers report that he sat for long interviews and supplied documents, and that other senior Apollo figures were interviewed too.

### How long is it?

The publisher lists 688 pages. The audiobook runs for more than 22 hours.
