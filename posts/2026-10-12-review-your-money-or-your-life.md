Your Money or Your Life was first published in 1992 and grew out of seminars given by Joe Dominguez. He died in 1997. Vicki Robin revised the book for a new edition in 2018, with a foreword by the blogger Mr. Money Mustache, and that is the edition reviewed here. It is one of the books behind the modern financial independence movement, and it starts from a different place than most money books: not how to earn more, but what your earning costs you.

## The short answer

A programme for changing how you think about spending, built on one idea: money is something you trade your life's hours for. The nine steps ask for honest, detailed record-keeping over months, and the book insists that every step be done. Readers who do the work often find it changes what they buy. The investing guidance is the weakest part and has had to be rewritten since the original, and the tone is closer to a way of life than to a finance manual. Read it for the spending side and get the investing side elsewhere.

## Who it is for

- People who earn enough and still feel short, and want to know where it goes.
- Readers drawn to financial independence who want its original text.
- Anyone who wants to connect spending with what they value.
- People who have tried budgets and given up on them.

## Who it is not for

- Readers who want a quick fix. The programme takes months of tracking.
- Readers looking mainly for investing detail.
- People who dislike a reflective, values-led tone, or who do not want environmental argument in a money book.

## How the book is organised

The publisher lists 368 pages. The book is a single programme of nine steps, meant to be done in order, with stories from people who followed it. The steps, as named in the summary on Vicki Robin's own website, are: making peace with the past; tracking your life energy; the monthly tabulation; three questions; making life energy visible; minimising spending; maximising income; capital and the crossover point; and managing your finances.

That summary was written for an earlier edition, and I have used it to check the detail below. The figures in the worked example are from that summary, and the 2018 edition's numbers may differ. The summary is firm on one point: each step depends on the ones before it, and the effect may not show until you have followed them for some months.

## Key ideas

### Money is life energy

The book rests on one definition: money is "something we choose to trade our life energy for." Life energy means your time, the stock of hours you have to live, which runs down and cannot be refilled. Everything else follows from taking that literally.

### Your real hourly wage

Step two asks what you actually earn per hour once the job's hidden costs are counted. You subtract from your pay what the job makes you spend: commuting, work clothes, meals at work, the spending you do to recover from it. You add to your hours the time the job takes outside working hours: travelling, getting ready, winding down.

The summary gives the book's example. A job paying $440 for a 40-hour week looks like $11 an hour. After adjustments it takes up 70 hours and leaves $280, or $4 an hour. For that person every dollar spent stands for about fifteen minutes of life. The figures are old; the method is the point.

### Track everything, then ask three questions

The second half of step two is to write down all money received and all money spent, to the cent. Step three sorts a month of those records into categories you choose yourself and restates each total in hours of work, using the real hourly wage.

Step four, which the summary calls the core of the programme, puts three questions to each category. Was what I got from this worth the hours of life it cost? Does this spending fit my values and what I want my life to be for? Would it look different if I no longer needed a job? You mark each category with a minus, a plus or a zero.

The summary is clear that this is not budgeting. It compares budgets to diets and says both fail for the same reason: they tackle the symptom and leave the cause alone. The claim is that spending falls by itself in the categories marked with a minus, once you can see what they cost.

### Enough

The word the programme is built around is "enough": the amount of money and possessions that leaves you most fulfilled, past which anything more is surplus. Frugality, as the book uses the word, is about getting full use and pleasure from what you already own. The summary's example is a wardrobe of ten dresses: the owner who still feels she has nothing to wear is the spendthrift, and the owner who has happily worn the same ten for years is the frugal one.

### The wall chart and the crossover point

Step five is to draw a large chart of each month's income and spending and put it up somewhere you cannot avoid looking at it. As savings build, step eight adds a third line: the monthly income your savings could produce. In the original programme this was worked out as your capital times the long-term interest rate of the day, divided by twelve. The crossover point is the month that line rises above the spending line. In the book's terms, that is financial independence: your needs are covered by income that does not come from a job.

*An illustration with round numbers, not taken from the book:* with savings of 300,000 and a long-term rate of 4%, the formula gives 300,000 × 0.04 ÷ 12 = 1,000 a month. If your spending is 2,500 a month, you are two-fifths of the way to the crossover point.

### How the investing step has changed

This is the part of the book that has moved most. Joe Dominguez's original criteria were strict. The money had to be completely secure, the income it paid had to be steady, and none of it was to go on commissions or fees. That led him to long-term United States government bonds. The summary notes that a later revision relaxed this to allow cautious, balanced holdings of mutual funds, property and other investments, and the publisher says the 2018 edition covers index funds.

## What works

- **The central idea changes behaviour.** Pricing a purchase in hours of work is simple and hard to forget.
- **It deals with spending without preaching a budget.** The publisher's own description sets attention and good habits against strict budgeting as the way to save.
- **Each step prepares the next,** and the wall chart gives quick, visible feedback.
- **It comes from practice.** The programme was taught in seminars for years before it became a book, and Dominguez had used it to leave paid work at 30.
- **The revision brought it up to date,** adding side income, online tracking and conversations about money.

## What to be aware of

- **The record-keeping is heavy.** Tracking every cent, every month, and re-asking the questions is more than many readers will keep up, and the book says half measures will not do.
- **The original investing advice has aged badly.** A plan built on living off the interest of long-term government bonds worked when those bonds paid high rates. At low rates the same formula demands far more capital, and a fixed income is exposed to inflation, which the original told readers not to fear too much. The publisher says the current edition covers index funds.
- **The crossover point is not a guarantee.** It depends on returns that vary, and on expenses staying where you expect.
- **Some steps assume United States records.** The summary itself notes that lifetime earnings history may be hard to obtain elsewhere.
- **The tone is earnest.** The book cares about consumption and the planet as much as about money, and it presents frugality as a moral good. Some readers will welcome that; others will find it preachy.
- **It suits people with room to cut.** A reader whose income barely covers essentials will find less to work with, although the book does include a step on raising income.

## How it compares

[The Simple Path to Wealth](../2026-09-07-review-the-simple-path-to-wealth/) comes from the same movement and is the mirror image: thin on spending, clear on investing. The two together make one complete book. [I Will Teach You to Be Rich](../2026-10-19-review-i-will-teach-you-to-be-rich/) takes the opposite view of spending: automate the important things and spend freely on what you love, without tracking every amount. Readers who find Robin's programme too demanding may do better there.

[The Psychology of Money](../2026-09-14-review-the-psychology-of-money/) makes a related point about wealth being what you do not spend, in a far lighter form and with no exercises.

## The verdict

Worth reading for steps two to four alone. Even a reader who never draws the wall chart is likely to look at spending differently afterwards. Treat step nine as an introduction and take your investing from a book written for that purpose.

## FAQ

### What are the nine steps?

Making peace with the past, tracking your life energy, the monthly tabulation, three questions, making life energy visible, minimising spending, maximising income, capital and the crossover point, and managing your finances.

### What is the crossover point?

The month when the income your savings could produce is higher than your expenses. In the book it marks financial independence.

### Is the 2018 edition very different from the original?

The nine steps are the same. The publisher says the revision adds index funds, side income, online tracking and conversations about money.

### Do I have to do all nine steps?

The book says yes, and in order. In practice many readers take the real hourly wage and the three questions and leave the rest, which still has value.
