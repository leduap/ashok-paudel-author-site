Most finance books treat money as a maths problem. Morgan Housel treats it as a behaviour problem. The Psychology of Money was published by Harriman House in September 2020. The publisher describes it as 19 short stories about the strange ways people think about money, and its opening claim is that doing well with money has less to do with what you know than with how you behave.

## The short answer

A short, well-written book about the human side of money: patience, luck, envy, fear and knowing when you have enough. It will not tell you which account to open or what to invest in. Read it for the way of thinking, and pair it with a practical guide for the steps.

## Who it is for

- New investors who want the right habits of mind before the technical detail.
- Experienced readers who know the theory and still find it hard to sit still in a falling market.
- Anyone who prefers stories to formulas.

## Who it is not for

- Readers who want a step-by-step plan, numbers or product comparisons.
- Readers who have already read a lot of behavioural finance and want new research.
- Anyone looking for a system to beat the market.

## Key ideas

The publisher's summary sets out the starting point: money is usually taught as a maths-based subject, but people do not make financial decisions on a spreadsheet. They make them at the dinner table or in a meeting room, where personal history, ego, pride, marketing and odd incentives are mixed together.

The chapter titles give a good map of where the book goes from there:

- **"No One's Crazy".** The opening chapter, on how each person's own history shapes their view of money.
- **"Luck & Risk"** and **"Never Enough".**
- **"Confounding Compounding".** On what long periods of time do to money.
- **"Getting Wealthy vs. Staying Wealthy".**
- **"Wealth is What You Don't See".** The idea, picked out by reviewers, that real wealth is invisible: it is what has been saved and invested, not what has been spent to be seen.
- **"Save Money".** Reviewers note the author's case for saving even without a specific goal.
- **"Reasonable > Rational"** and **"Room for Error".**
- **"The Seduction of Pessimism"** and **"When You'll Believe Anything".**

Near the end come a chapter titled "Confessions" and a postscript on the history of how the American consumer came to think the way they do.

## What works

- **The central point is true and useful.** Many investing mistakes are mistakes of behaviour, and the book makes that vivid.
- **It is easy to read.** The chapters are short and each can be read on its own.
- **It suits readers at any level,** because it assumes no technical knowledge.

## What to be aware of

- **It is not a how-to guide.** There are no instructions on accounts, funds or budgets.
- **Stories are not evidence.** The examples are chosen to make a point, and the argument is persuasive more than it is tested.
- **The lessons overlap.** With around twenty short chapters on one theme, some ground is covered more than once.
- **The examples are mostly American,** including the postscript.

## The verdict

A good book to read early and to reread when markets are frightening. Its lessons are about temperament, and they are the ones that are hardest to learn from a spreadsheet. For a practical companion, the reviews of [The Simple Path to Wealth](../2026-09-07-review-the-simple-path-to-wealth/) and [I Will Teach You to Be Rich](../2026-10-19-review-i-will-teach-you-to-be-rich/) cover two books that tell you what to do.

## FAQ

### Is The Psychology of Money good for beginners?

Yes. It assumes no knowledge of finance. It will shape how a beginner thinks, but it does not explain how to start investing.

### How long is it?

The publisher lists 256 pages for the paperback. The chapters are short.

### Does it give investment advice?

Not in the sense of telling you what to buy. It is about how people think and behave around money.
