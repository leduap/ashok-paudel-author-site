The Intelligent Investor was first published in 1949. Benjamin Graham revised it several times, the last in 1971 and 1972, and he died in 1976. The edition reviewed here is the revised edition of 2003, which keeps Graham's final text, carries a preface and an appendix by Warren Buffett, and adds commentary and footnotes by the journalist Jason Zweig. Buffett's line on the cover calls it "by far the best book on investing ever written."

## The short answer

A book to read for its way of thinking, not for its instructions. Three ideas are as useful now as when they were written: a share is part of a business, the market's daily prices are offers you are free to ignore, and you should never pay so much that you depend on everything going right. Much of the rest, including the bond discussion, the tax rules and the company examples, describes the American market of the early 1970s. It is long and demanding, and even Graham's most famous pupil says its methods are not enough on their own today.

## Who it is for

- Readers who want to understand where value investing comes from, in the words of the person who taught it.
- Investors who want a calmer way to think about falling prices.
- Readers willing to work through an old book with a modern guide alongside.
- Index-fund investors who want to hear the strongest version of the other side.

## Who it is not for

- Beginners who want to be told what to do this year.
- Readers looking for a stock-picking formula that can be applied as written today.
- Anyone short of time: the revised edition runs to about 640 pages.

## How the book is organised

The 2003 edition has an introduction, twenty chapters and a postscript, each followed by Zweig's commentary, and then a set of appendixes.

The chapters fall into rough groups. The first three set the ground: investment versus speculation, inflation, and a century of stock market history up to early 1972. Chapters four to seven give portfolio policy, first for the "defensive" investor and then for the "enterprising" one. Chapter eight is "The Investor and Market Fluctuations", and chapters nine and ten cover investment funds and advisers. Chapters eleven to nineteen are the working part: security analysis for the lay investor, per-share earnings, stock selection for each type of investor, convertible issues, and several chapters comparing real companies of the time side by side. Chapter twenty is "'Margin of Safety' as the Central Concept of Investment".

The appendixes include Buffett's essay "The Superinvestors of Graham-and-Doddsville" and notes on United States investment tax as of 1972 and 2003.

## Key ideas

### Investment versus speculation

Graham insists the reader begin by separating the two. His definition, first given in his earlier book Security Analysis, is that an investment operation is one which, "upon thorough analysis, promises safety of principal and a satisfactory return", and that operations not meeting these requirements are speculative. Three tests are packed into that sentence: you have done the analysis, you are protected against serious loss, and you are aiming for an adequate return, not a spectacular one.

Behind it is the view that a share is first of all part-ownership of a business. In the short term, in his well-known phrase, the market behaves like a voting machine; in the long term it acts like a weighing machine.

### The defensive and the enterprising investor

Graham writes separate chapters for two kinds of reader. The defensive investor wants to keep time, effort and, above all, worry to a minimum, and so trades seldom, gives up trying to forecast prices and holds for the long term. The enterprising investor has more time and interest and is prepared to do original analysis in search of exceptional bargains.

The distinction is about effort, not about appetite for risk. Graham's warning is that the enterprising path demands substantial work, and that there is little middle ground where a bit of effort earns a bit of extra return.

### Mr. Market

In the chapter on market fluctuations, Graham asks the reader to imagine a business partner, Mr. Market, who turns up every day offering to buy your share or sell you his. Usually the price he names seems plausible; occasionally it is ridiculous. You are free to trade with him or to ignore him completely. He does not mind, and will be back tomorrow with another price.

The point is that a price is an offer, not a verdict on what a business is worth. The investor should profit from the market's folly instead of taking part in it, and should watch how the businesses perform, not how Mr. Market behaves. It is the most practical idea in the book, because it changes what a falling price means: for someone who is still buying, it is a better offer.

### Margin of safety

The margin of safety is the gap between what a careful estimate says a share is worth and the price paid for it. Because any estimate of value is rough, the investor insists on a wide gap, so that mistakes and bad luck can be absorbed. Graham's own line is that the margin of safety is always dependent on the price paid: a sound company can be a poor investment if bought too dear.

Buffett's comparison helps: you build a bridge to carry 30,000 pounds and then drive 10,000-pound trucks across it.

*An illustration with round numbers, not taken from the book:* if your careful estimate of a company's worth is 100 per share and you buy at 65, your estimate can be wrong by a third before you have overpaid. If you buy at 95, almost any error leaves you with a loss.

### Think for yourself, and be businesslike

Graham writes that "investment is most intelligent when it is most businesslike", meaning a systematic effort to earn a reasonable return and avoid severe loss. He adds that agreement or disagreement from the crowd proves nothing: "You are right because your data and reasoning are right." He was also critical of companies that reported their finances obscurely, favoured paying dividends over hoarding profits, and distrusted the claim that some shares were worth buying at any price because of their growth.

## What works

- **The central ideas are durable.** Mr. Market and the margin of safety are still the clearest explanations of how to behave when prices swing.
- **It is honest about effort.** Graham does not pretend that beating the market is easy, and he steers most readers towards the defensive approach.
- **It teaches by worked comparison.** The chapters that set real companies side by side show the method in use, not only in principle.
- **The commentary helps.** Zweig relates each chapter to later events. Without it, a modern reader would struggle with many of the examples.
- **Buffett's appendix answers the obvious objection.** It presents the records of nine funds run on Graham's principles as evidence that their results were not luck.

## What to be aware of

- **Much of the detail is dated.** The securities, yields and tax rules discussed are those of the United States around 1972, and one chapter is fixed on the level of share prices in that year.
- **It is hard going.** The prose is formal and several chapters are close studies of company accounts.
- **Its best-known followers say it is not sufficient.** Buffett and his partner Charlie Munger have both said Graham's methods are necessary but not enough for investing today, because Graham placed too little weight on a company's potential for future growth.
- **Graham's own view shifted.** Late in life he described moving away from detailed analysis of single companies towards buying groups of shares that met simple tests of cheapness.
- **The evidence for the method is contested.** Supporters of efficient markets argue that the few investors who beat the averages may simply be lucky. Buffett's appendix is a reply to exactly that argument, and readers should hear both sides.
- **There is a newer edition.** A third revised edition with updated commentary was published in 2024. The edition linked on this page is the 2003 revision.
- **It is not a system.** Readers who come for rules to apply mechanically will be disappointed.

## How it compares

[A Random Walk Down Wall Street](../2026-09-28-review-a-random-walk-down-wall-street/) is the natural opponent: Malkiel tests the kind of company analysis Graham teaches and concludes most investors should not attempt it. The two agree on more than their reputations suggest, since Graham also tells most readers to take the defensive path. [The Little Book of Common Sense Investing](../2026-09-21-review-the-little-book-of-common-sense-investing/) has a whole chapter asking what Graham would have thought of index funds.

[The Psychology of Money](../2026-09-14-review-the-psychology-of-money/) restates Graham's lessons on temperament, and borrows the margin of safety directly, in a book a tenth as hard to read. Start there if this one feels heavy.

## The verdict

Read the chapter on market fluctuations and the chapter on margin of safety closely, read the defensive investor chapters next, and treat the rest as background to dip into. As a guide to behaviour it has not been bettered. As a manual for choosing shares it belongs to its time, and most readers will end up at the same practical conclusion as the index-fund books.

## FAQ

### Which edition should I read?

The revised edition with Jason Zweig's commentary is the one most readers use. It was published in 2003, and an updated third revision followed in 2024.

### Is The Intelligent Investor suitable for beginners?

It can be read by a beginner, but it is not the easiest starting point. A shorter modern book first, and this one second, is a gentler order.

### Who was Benjamin Graham?

An investor and teacher who began teaching his approach at Columbia Business School in 1928 and co-wrote Security Analysis in 1934. Warren Buffett is the best known of his students.

### What is value investing, in one sentence?

Buying shares for clearly less than a careful estimate of what the underlying business is worth, and waiting.
