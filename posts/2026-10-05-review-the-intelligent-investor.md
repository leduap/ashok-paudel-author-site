The Intelligent Investor was first published in 1949. Benjamin Graham revised it several times, the last in 1971 and 1972, and he died in 1976. The edition reviewed here is the revised edition of 2003, which keeps Graham's final text, carries a preface and an appendix by Warren Buffett, and adds commentary and footnotes by the journalist Jason Zweig. Buffett's line on the cover calls it "by far the best book on investing ever written."

## The short answer

A book to read for its way of thinking, not for its instructions. Two ideas are as useful now as when they were written: treat the market's daily prices as offers you are free to ignore, and never pay so much that you depend on everything going right. Much of the rest, including the bond tables and company examples, describes the American market of the early 1970s. It is long and demanding.

## Who it is for

- Readers who want to understand where value investing comes from, in the words of the person who taught it.
- Investors who want a calmer way to think about falling prices.
- Readers willing to work through an old book with a modern guide alongside.

## Who it is not for

- Beginners who want to be told what to do this year.
- Readers looking for a stock-picking formula that can be applied as written today.
- Anyone short of time: the revised edition runs to about 640 pages.

## Key ideas

- **Investment versus speculation.** The first chapter draws the line between the two and sets out what results an investor can reasonably expect.
- **The defensive and the enterprising investor.** Graham writes separate chapters on portfolio policy and on stock selection for each. The defensive investor wants safety and little effort; the enterprising investor is prepared to put in real work. Most people, he suggests, belong in the first group.
- **Mr. Market.** In the chapter on market fluctuations, Graham asks the reader to imagine a business partner who turns up every day offering to buy your share or sell you his, at a price that is sometimes sensible and sometimes absurd. You are free to accept or to ignore him. The point is that a price is an offer, not a verdict on what a business is worth.
- **Margin of safety.** The final chapter is titled "'Margin of Safety' as the Central Concept of Investment". The idea is to buy only when the price is far enough below a careful estimate of value that mistakes and bad luck can be absorbed.
- **Inflation, funds and advisers.** There are chapters on each, along with case histories of companies of the time.

Zweig's commentary follows each chapter and relates Graham's points to later events. An appendix reprints Buffett's essay "The Superinvestors of Graham-and-Doddsville".

## What works

- **The two central ideas are durable.** Mr. Market and the margin of safety are still the clearest explanations of how to behave when prices swing.
- **It is honest about effort.** Graham does not pretend that beating the market is easy, and he steers most readers towards the defensive approach.
- **The commentary helps.** Without it, a modern reader would struggle with many of the examples.

## What to be aware of

- **Much of the detail is dated.** The securities, yields and tax rules discussed are those of the United States around 1972.
- **It is hard going.** The prose is formal and several chapters are close studies of company accounts.
- **Graham's own view shifted.** Later in life he described moving away from detailed analysis of single companies towards buying groups of shares that met simple tests.
- **There is a newer edition.** A third revised edition with updated commentary was published in 2024. The edition linked on this page is the 2003 revision.
- **It is not a system.** Readers who come for rules to apply mechanically will be disappointed.

## The verdict

Read the chapter on market fluctuations and the chapter on margin of safety, and treat the rest as background to dip into. For most readers the practical conclusion points the same way as the index-fund books reviewed here, such as [The Little Book of Common Sense Investing](../2026-09-21-review-the-little-book-of-common-sense-investing/), which has a chapter asking what Graham would have thought of indexing.

## FAQ

### Which edition should I read?

The revised edition with Jason Zweig's commentary is the one most readers use. It was published in 2003, and an updated third revision followed in 2024.

### Is The Intelligent Investor suitable for beginners?

It can be read by a beginner, but it is not the easiest starting point. A shorter modern book first, and this one second, is a gentler order.

### Who was Benjamin Graham?

An investor and teacher who began teaching his approach at Columbia Business School in 1928. Warren Buffett is the best known of his students.
