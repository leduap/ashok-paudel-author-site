Financial Intelligence, written by Karen Berman and Joe Knight with John Case, is aimed at managers who sit in meetings full of numbers and are not sure what all of them mean. It was first published in 2006 and later revised.

## What the book covers

The book explains the three main financial statements in turn: the income statement, the balance sheet and the cash flow statement. It then covers ratios, return on investment and the management of working capital.

What sets it apart is its starting point, which the authors call **the art of finance**. Financial statements look exact, but they depend on estimates and assumptions: when revenue is recognised, how long an asset is expected to last, how much of what customers owe will actually be paid. Two honest accountants can reach different figures from the same facts.

A second theme is that **profit is not cash**. A company can report a profit and still run out of money, because of the gap between when a sale is recorded and when the customer pays.

## What works

- **It explains why, not only what.** Understanding the judgement behind a number makes you a better reader of it.
- **It is written for non-specialists.** Terms are defined in plain language, in boxes beside the text.
- **The profit and cash distinction is handled well.** This is the point most new managers get wrong, and the book returns to it often enough to make it stick.
- **Chapters are short** and can be read as a reference.

## What to be aware of

- **It is an introduction.** Readers who work in finance will find it basic, though it is a good book to give to colleagues in other functions.
- **It follows accounting rules in the United States.** The concepts carry over to international standards, but some terms and treatments differ.
- **It describes; it does not train.** You will understand a set of accounts better, but there is little practice material.

## Who should read it

Managers, founders and anyone who has been handed a budget without an explanation. It is also useful for people starting in finance who want the bigger picture before the detail.
