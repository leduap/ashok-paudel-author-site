Financial Intelligence, written by Karen Berman and Joe Knight with John Case, is aimed at managers who sit in meetings full of numbers and are not sure what all of them mean. It was first released in 2006. The edition reviewed here is the revised edition, published by Harvard Business Review Press in 2013.

## The short answer

A plain, well-organised introduction to the three main financial statements and the ratios built from them, written for people who are not accountants. Its most valuable idea is that reported numbers depend on assumptions and estimates, so reading them well means knowing where the judgement sits. It is an introduction and follows United States accounting. People who work in finance will find it basic.

## Who it is for

- Managers who have been given a budget or a set of results without an explanation.
- Founders and owners who want to follow a conversation with their accountant.
- People starting in finance who want the whole picture before the detail.

## Who it is not for

- Qualified accountants and experienced finance staff.
- Readers who want exercises and practice problems.
- Readers who need the detail of international accounting standards.

## Key ideas

The authors' own description of the book lists five areas:

- **The basics of financial measurement.** How to read an income statement, a balance sheet and a cash flow statement.
- **"The art of finance".** Separating hard data from assumptions and estimates, and understanding where the numbers come from. This is the idea that sets the book apart from a plain primer.
- **The mechanics of analysis.** Calculating ratios, return on investment and working capital.
- **Cash and profit.** How the two differ, and why cash gets so much attention.
- **Financial literacy and transparency.** The argument that a company performs better when more of its people understand the numbers.

The publisher's description of the revised edition says it brings the numbers up to date and adds discussion of the financial crisis and of financial and accounting literacy more broadly.

## What works

- **It explains why, not only what.** Knowing that a figure rests on an estimate makes you a better reader of it.
- **It is written for non-specialists.** The authors describe it as jargon-free and built around stories from real companies.
- **The difference between profit and cash is given its own place.** It is one of the points new managers most often get wrong.
- **It comes out of teaching.** The authors say it is based on training managers and employees, and it reads that way.

## What to be aware of

- **It is an introduction.** It will help you read a set of accounts, not prepare one.
- **It follows accounting practice in the United States.** The concepts carry over elsewhere, but some terms and treatments differ.
- **The edition dates from 2013.** Accounting standards have changed in places since then.
- **The authors run a training business** built on the same material, which is worth knowing when reading their own description of the book.
- **It describes more than it drills.** There is little practice material.

## The verdict

A good book to give a colleague outside finance, and a sound first read for anyone who wants to stop nodding along in budget meetings. Readers who want to apply the same statement-reading to buying a company may find the review of [Buy Then Build](../2026-11-02-review-buy-then-build/) useful.

## FAQ

### Do I need an accounting background to read it?

No. It is written for managers who are not accountants.

### Which edition is this?

The revised edition of 2013, 284 pages according to its Open Library record. The book first came out in 2006.

### Who are the authors?

Karen Berman and Joe Knight, of the Business Literacy Institute, a financial training company. John Case is credited on the cover as co-writer.
