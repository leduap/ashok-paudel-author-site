Financial Intelligence, written by Karen Berman and Joe Knight with John Case, is aimed at managers who sit in meetings full of numbers and are not sure what all of them mean. It was first released in 2006. The edition reviewed here is the revised edition, published by Harvard Business Review Press in 2013.

## The short answer

A plain, well-organised introduction to the three main financial statements and the ratios built from them, written for people who are not accountants. Its most valuable idea is that reported numbers depend on rules, estimates and assumptions, so reading them well means knowing where the judgement sits and being willing to ask about it. It is an introduction, it follows United States accounting, it dates from 2013, and it gives little practice. People who work in finance will find it basic, though useful to hand to colleagues who do not.

## Who it is for

- Managers who have been given a budget or a set of results without an explanation.
- Founders and owners who want to follow a conversation with their accountant.
- People starting in finance who want the whole picture before the detail.
- Finance staff looking for a way to explain their work to the rest of the business.

## Who it is not for

- Qualified accountants and experienced finance staff, for their own learning.
- Readers who want exercises and practice problems.
- Readers who need the detail of international accounting standards.
- Investors looking for a guide to analysing shares. The book is written from inside a company, not from the stock market.

## How the book is organised

The Open Library record gives 284 pages. I have not seen the full contents page, so this outline is built from the excerpts the authors publish on their training company's website, which name several chapters and parts.

The book works through the statements in order. The income statement comes early (chapter 6 is "Cracking the Code of the Income Statement" and chapter 7 is on revenue), the balance sheet follows (chapter 10 is "Understanding Balance Sheet Basics"), and cash comes after that (chapter 17 is "The Language of Cash Flow"). Later material covers ratios, return on investment and working capital. At least some parts end with a "toolbox" of short practical notes; an excerpt on one profit measure comes from the "Part Four Toolbox".

The authors' own description lists five areas: the basics of financial measurement, the art of finance, the mechanics of analysis, cash and profit, and the case for financial literacy across a company.

## Key ideas

### Financial intelligence is a set of skills

The book's opening claim, in a published excerpt, is that financial intelligence "isn't some innate ability". It is a set of skills that people in finance learn early and that other managers too often never pick up. The authors name four: understanding the foundation (reading the three statements and knowing why the balance sheet balances), understanding the art, understanding analysis, and understanding the big picture, meaning that numbers never tell the whole story without the economy, competitors and customers around them.

### The art of finance

This is the idea that sets the book apart from a plain primer. In the authors' words, finance and accounting "are an art as well as a science": they must put numbers on things that cannot always be measured exactly, and so rely on rules, estimates and assumptions. A financially intelligent manager can see where that judgement has been applied and knows how applying it differently might lead to a different conclusion.

Their example is revenue. A company normally records a sale when it delivers the product or service, but "when is a sale a sale" is often unclear. A firm that is paid over the life of a building project, or a software company that sells five years of upgrades with its product, must decide how much to count now. The chapter describes how a company under pressure to meet a profit forecast could change that proportion and lift its reported earnings, and notes that such a change is not illegal.

### How to read an income statement

The advice is practical. Every income statement has sales at the top, costs and expenses in the middle and profit at the bottom. To see what matters to a company, look for the biggest numbers relative to sales: if the cost of goods sold is a large share, management will be watching it closely.

### Profit is not cash

The authors give this its own section, and their site sets out the three reasons. Revenue is counted at the time of sale, before the customer has paid. Expenses are matched to the revenue they help earn, not to the date the bill is settled. And spending on equipment does not reduce profit when the money goes out; only a yearly slice, called depreciation, is charged. The conclusion is blunt: a company can be profitable and still run out of cash.

*An illustration with round numbers, not taken from the book:* a firm sells 100,000 of goods in March on 60 days' credit, having paid 70,000 for stock and wages that month. March shows a profit of 30,000. Its bank balance is down 70,000, and stays down until May.

### The balance sheet as a running total

The balance sheet shows what a company owns, what it owes and what is left for the owners on one particular day. The book starts from a person's net worth and then swaps the vocabulary. Its analogy for how the statements connect is a student's marks: profit for a period is like the grade for one course, and the owners' equity on the balance sheet is like the overall average, which every grade moves but none decides.

### Cash flow, and a warning about shortcuts

The cash flow statement sorts cash into three kinds: from operations, from investing and from financing. The authors call operating cash flow in many ways the single most important number for the health of a business, because a company that generates it can pay for more of its own growth.

They are cautious about popular short-cut measures. EBITDA, a profit figure before interest, tax and depreciation that lenders like as a rough guide to cash, depends on the income statement, and the excerpt notes that after the accounting scandals around 2000 and the crisis of 2008 investors grew wary of it for that reason. On return on investment, the hard part is not the formula but the forecast: most investments that fail were justified by unrealistic estimates of future benefits.

## What works

- **It explains why, not only what.** Knowing that a figure rests on an estimate makes you a better reader of it.
- **It encourages questions.** The authors say outright that they want readers to look at reports "with a questioning eye".
- **It is written for non-specialists.** One reviewer quoted on the authors' site liked that the words debit and credit never appear.
- **The analogies are good.** Net worth for the balance sheet and grades for profit and equity stay in the memory.
- **It comes out of teaching.** The authors train managers for a living, and the book reads that way.

## What to be aware of

- **It is an introduction.** It will help you read a set of accounts, not prepare one, and it stops well short of what a finance team does.
- **It follows accounting practice in the United States.** The concepts carry over elsewhere, but some terms and treatments differ.
- **The edition dates from 2013.** Accounting standards have changed in places since then, so check current rules before relying on a specific treatment. The principle that judgement is involved still holds.
- **The authors run a training business** built on the same material, and most of what can be checked about the book online comes from that business.
- **It describes more than it drills.** There is little practice material, so readers who learn by doing will need real accounts to work on.
- **The publisher's own page could not be checked.** The description of what the revised edition added comes from the library record.

## How it compares

Among the books reviewed here it is the only one about company accounts. [Buy Then Build](../2026-11-02-review-buy-then-build/) assumes this knowledge: anyone weighing up a business for sale needs to read its statements and to know that profit and cash differ. [The Intelligent Investor](../2026-10-05-review-the-intelligent-investor/) has chapters on per-share earnings and on comparing companies that make far more sense after this book.

Against standard accounting textbooks its advantage is length and tone; its disadvantage is the lack of exercises.

## The verdict

A good book to give a colleague outside finance, and a sound first read for anyone who wants to stop nodding along in budget meetings. Read the chapters on the art of finance and on cash most closely. Then pick up your own company's statements and ask where the estimates are.

## FAQ

### Do I need an accounting background to read it?

No. It is written for managers who are not accountants.

### Which edition is this?

The revised edition of 2013, 284 pages according to its Open Library record. The book first came out in 2006.

### Who are the authors?

Karen Berman and Joe Knight, of the Business Literacy Institute, a financial training company. John Case is credited on the cover as co-writer.

### What is the difference between profit and cash?

Profit counts sales when they are made and matches costs to them. Cash counts money when it actually arrives or leaves. The timing gap is why a profitable company can run short of cash.
