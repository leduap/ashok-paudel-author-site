Strangers: A Memoir of Marriage is Belle Burden's account of the sudden end of her twenty-year marriage and what she found when she looked back over it. The Dial Press published it on 13 January 2026. It has been on the New York Times hardcover nonfiction list for most of the year, and stood at number 7 on the list dated 13 September 2026, its 32nd week.

## The short answer

This is a personal memoir about betrayal and recovery. It is reviewed on a finance site because much of the public conversation about it has been about money: a prenuptial agreement signed against a lawyer's advice, property put in joint names, and a wife who by her own description stayed out of the household finances. As a prompt to ask who owns what in a marriage, it has clearly worked on many readers. As a guide it has limits. It is one person's telling of a dispute with a living former spouse who has not given his version, the author's circumstances are far wealthier than most, and reporting in the New Yorker has suggested the financial danger was less stark than the book implies.

## Who it is for

- Readers who want a well-told, first-person account of a marriage ending without warning.
- Anyone in a long partnership who has left the money to the other person and has been meaning to change that.
- People about to marry who are discussing, or avoiding, a prenuptial agreement.
- Advisers and lawyers who want to know the book their clients are quoting.

## Who it is not for

- Readers who want practical, step-by-step guidance on divorce and money. This is a story, not a handbook.
- Anyone looking for a balanced account of the marriage. Only one spouse is speaking.
- Readers who will find it hard to sympathise with financial worry at this level of wealth.
- Readers outside the United States hoping for legal detail that applies to them. The law described is American and specific to the couple's own contract.

## How the book is organised

The publisher lists 256 pages. No chapter list was available on the publisher's page, so this review does not describe one. From the publisher's description and the reviews, the book begins in March 2020 at the family's house on Martha's Vineyard, in the first weeks of the pandemic, when the author learned of her husband's affair and he left the next morning. It then moves backwards through the marriage in search of signs she had missed, takes in her own family history and what it taught her about how a wife was expected to behave, and follows the divorce and her life afterwards. The Observer's reviewer describes the method as a lawyer re-examining her own case. The husband is given a different name in the book.

The memoir grew out of an essay the author wrote for the New York Times in 2023. This review is written from the publisher's details and the published coverage listed under Sources.

## Key ideas

### A marriage reread from the end

The emotional subject is whether one can know another person. The author thought her marriage was happy. After it ended in a day, she went back over two decades asking what had been visible all along. That re-reading is what gives the book its shape and its title, and reviewers agree it is the part that has connected with the widest audience.

### The prenuptial agreement

A prenuptial agreement is a contract signed before marriage that sets out who keeps what if the marriage ends. According to the New Yorker's account of the book, the author's fiancé asked for a clause under which each spouse's income and investments during the marriage would stay separate unless deliberately placed in both names. Her own lawyer advised against it and she signed anyway. CNBC's report on the book notes the consequence: when the marriage ended, his earnings over twenty years were outside the division.

### Joint names on what one person paid for

The second decision compounded the first. The author writes that she used her inherited trust money to buy the family's two homes and then put both in joint names, because she believed that sharing was what marriage meant. Under the contract, jointly held property was the one category to be split.

*An illustration with round numbers, not taken from the book:* one spouse pays 1,000 for a house from her own funds and registers it in both names. The other spouse keeps 2,000 of earnings in an account in his name alone. If their agreement says only jointly held assets are shared, a separation leaves her with a claim to 500 of the house and him with 500 of the house plus his 2,000. She paid for the whole of the only asset that is divided.

The point for any reader is that the name on the title can matter more than who wrote the cheque.

### Not looking

The author is frank that she did not involve herself in the couple's money. CNBC quotes her description of an unspoken division of labour in which he worked and she raised the children, and her admission that she preferred comfort to conflict and not knowing to knowing. She had trained as a lawyer and still stepped back. The advisers CNBC interviewed say they see the same pattern at every level of income. This is the most transferable idea in the book: disengagement is a decision, made in small steps, and its cost appears only when something breaks.

### From compliance to speaking

The publisher frames the book as a change in the author herself, from someone with a reputation for being good and discreet to someone prepared to tell the story in public. Whatever one makes of the financial details, the book is also about deciding to stop keeping quiet.

## What works

- **It has made people check their own position.** A memoir has reached readers that a personal finance guide would not, and CNBC and others have used it to explain titling, separate property and prenuptial terms.
- **The author owns her mistakes.** She does not present herself only as a victim. She says plainly that she ignored legal advice and chose not to look.
- **It shows that money knowledge is not the same as money attention.** A qualified lawyer from a financially sophisticated family still ended up surprised, which is a more useful warning than one aimed at the uninformed.
- **It is short and readable.** At 256 pages it does not outstay its welcome.

## What to be aware of

- **It is one side of a dispute.** The former husband is a living person who appears under another name and has not published a reply. The New Yorker reports that he did not respond to its requests for comment. Readers should hold the portrait of him loosely.
- **The financial peril has been questioned.** The New Yorker's Jessica Winter obtained court documents and reported that the author had substantial inherited interests and income of her own, and that some figures in the book, such as the size of a mortgage, differ from public records. A spokesperson for the author is quoted in reply on one point. The lesson about joint names stands; the degree of danger is contested.
- **Lawyers disagree about the contract.** The same article quotes one law professor who regards keeping incomes separate as a common and defensible arrangement, and another who thinks a partner who raised the children should share in the earnings. The book presents the agreement as plainly unfair, and that is a view, not a settled fact.
- **The setting is very privileged.** Two expensive homes and family trusts are a long way from most readers' lives. The principles carry over; the sums do not.
- **It gives no instructions.** Anyone facing these decisions needs advice on their own law and their own documents. A memoir cannot supply that.

## How it compares

There is no close equivalent among the reviews on this site. [I Will Teach You to Be Rich](../2026-10-19-review-i-will-teach-you-to-be-rich/) is the practical counterpart: it tells a reader how to set up accounts and know where the money is, which is exactly what the author says she did not do. [The Psychology of Money](../2026-09-14-review-the-psychology-of-money/) deals in general terms with the way behaviour, more than knowledge, drives decisions about money. [The Glass Castle](../2026-09-05-review-the-glass-castle/) is another memoir in which family and money are tangled together, from the opposite end of the income scale.

A screen adaptation has been announced, according to Wikipedia. The Dial Press hardcover is the edition on the list.

## The verdict

Read Strangers as a memoir about a marriage, and treat the money as its most discussed thread, not as its purpose. The useful conclusion is modest and does not depend on the disputed details: know what you have signed, know whose name is on what, and do not hand the whole subject to someone else.

## FAQ

### Is Strangers a personal finance book?

No. It is a memoir of a marriage and a divorce. It has been widely discussed for what it shows about prenuptial agreements and about one partner staying out of the finances.

### Is the financial story in the book accurate?

It is the author's own account. The New Yorker, after reviewing court documents, reported that the picture was more complicated than the book and much of the coverage suggested.

### What is a prenuptial agreement?

A contract made before marriage that sets out how money and property will be divided if the marriage ends. Its effect depends on its exact wording and on local law.

### How long is the book?

The publisher lists 256 pages.
