At the manufacturer where I run global controlling, nobody is allowed to choose a supplier on the price per part. A quote that looks cheap on unit price can turn expensive once freight, duties, packaging, surcharges and payment terms are added. So we compare landed cost: one number, everything in. A buyer who picked a supplier on the headline price alone would have some explaining to do.

Consumers have never had that discipline, and much of the economy is built on the fact. Concert tickets, hotel rooms, apartments, flights: the price you see first is rarely the price you pay. The fees arrive at checkout, after you have invested time and made up your mind.

It works remarkably well. In a 2015 experiment on StubHub, half of U.S. users saw ticket prices with fees included from the start; the other half saw the fees only at checkout. The second group spent about 21 percent more and was 14 percent more likely to buy, according to a [study published in Marketing Science](https://vcresearch.berkeley.edu/news/buyer-beware-massive-experiment-shows-why-ticket-sellers-hit-you-last-second-fees). Hiding the fees did more than lift revenue. It made comparison harder, so people bought pricier seats.

Regulators have been chasing this one industry at a time. Since May 2025, a [Federal Trade Commission rule](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2025/05/ftc-rule-unfair-or-deceptive-fees-take-effect-may-12-2025) has required sellers of live-event tickets and short-term lodging to show the total price upfront. Last December, Greystar, the country's largest apartment manager, [agreed to pay $24 million](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2025/12/greystar-agrees-pay-24-million-stop-deceptive-advertising-practices-result-ftc-colorado-lawsuit) and to advertise total monthly rent including mandatory fees. Each fix is welcome, and each takes years.

Something faster is arriving. This holiday season, [Adobe expects](https://business.adobe.com/resources/holiday-shopping-report.html) traffic from AI assistants to U.S. retail sites to rise 130 percent from last year. Shoppers are letting chatbots compare and, increasingly, buy. OpenAI and Stripe launched checkout inside ChatGPT last year on an [open protocol](https://stripe.com/newsroom/news/stripe-openai-instant-checkout) designed for AI agents that purchase on a person's behalf.

Drip pricing exploits human limits. We anchor on the first number, we tire, and we hate starting over after 20 minutes of clicking. Software has none of these weaknesses. An agent told to find two concert seats for under $300, all in, does not care how low the advertised price looked. It can add up the fees on every option and walk away from the one that hides them, without the sunk-cost regret that keeps a person on the checkout page. To an agent, the headline price is noise. The total is the only price.

That should change how companies think about fees. The business case for drip pricing rests on attention, and attention is exactly what consumers are delegating. A merchant whose fees an agent cannot see before checkout is not winning a smarter sale. It risks being dropped from the shortlist. The winners will be firms that publish their full price, base fare and mandatory fees and genuine options, as clean data a machine can read.

That is not the same as banning unbundling. An airline that charges for checked bags deceives no one if it says so plainly. U.S. carriers collected [$7.4 billion](https://www.bts.gov/newsroom/us-airlines-profited-60-billion-2025-decrease-over-2024) in baggage fees last year, and a traveler with only a backpack is better off not paying for everyone else's suitcase. An agent that knows you are checking one bag can price that trip to the dollar. What it cannot price is a fee it never sees.

Today, it often can't. In [Adobe's analysis](https://business.adobe.com/assets/pdfs/resources/sdk/ai-traffic-trends-report-august-2026/ai-traffic-trends-report-2026-08-19.pdf) of how readable websites are to AI, booking and checkout pages scored lowest of any page type in all three industries studied. The place where fees have traditionally appeared is exactly where the machines see worst.

That gap matters. If the total price lives only at checkout, agents will keep ranking options on the advertised number, and drip pricing will simply move from fooling people to fooling their software. If the total travels with the product data from the start, the agent becomes every household's procurement department.

So the most useful step is not another sector-by-sector rule about what a webpage must display. It is a simple standard for what the data must contain: a mandatory, machine-readable total price for every offer, with optional extras declared separately. The open protocols that AI companies, payment firms and retailers are writing right now are the cheapest place to set that norm, while the ink is wet.

Businesses that spent a decade engineering the checkout page should notice where their customers are going. Soon the shopper reading your price may not be a person at all. It will not get tired, it will not feel committed, and it will add up everything. Price accordingly.
