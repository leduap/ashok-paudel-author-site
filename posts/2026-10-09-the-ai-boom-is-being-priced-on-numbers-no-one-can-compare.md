On Oct. 8, a definitional question knocked billions of dollars off the market value of companies whose own books had not changed. OpenAI told investors that its annualized revenue had reached roughly $50 billion at the end of September (CNBC, Oct. 8). Late last month the widely reported figure had been $68 billion to $70 billion. Nothing had collapsed. The higher number had been "grossed up" to count sales through cloud partners the way OpenAI's rival Anthropic counts them, so that investors could compare the two (Axios, Oct. 8). On the news, Oracle fell nearly 6 percent, CoreWeave nearly 8 percent and Nvidia 3 percent (CNBC).

Both companies' methods comply with generally accepted accounting principles (Axios). That is the problem.

I work in corporate finance in Munich, and my working life has turned on one unglamorous question: What does a reported number actually mean? Whenever a company moves its finance teams onto a single system, the hardest part is not the software. It is getting an accountant in one country and another somewhere else to book the same transaction the same way. Until they do, a figure can be precise, fully reconciled and still not comparable. The A.I. industry now has that problem at the scale of the stock market.

The rules that govern revenue, ASC 606 in the United States and its international twin, IFRS 15, were issued together in May 2014 with an explicit promise to "improve comparability of revenue recognition practices across entities, industries, jurisdictions, and capital markets" (FASB, ASU 2014-09). They are good standards. But they hinge on a judgment about control. A company reports the full price a customer pays only if it controls the product before the customer gets it; otherwise it reports only its cut (ASC 606-10-55-36 through 55-40). When a business buys a frontier model through a cloud marketplace, who controls the product: the lab that trained the model, or the cloud provider that hosts it, bills for it and owns the customer relationship? The two leading labs, reading the same paragraphs in good faith, have landed on opposite answers for their partner sales (Axios). Each answer can be audited. Neither can be compared with the other.

Newer pricing makes it worse. Customers prepay for blocks of tokens and credits they may never use, leaving each company to judge how much of that unused "breakage" to book as revenue, and when (ASC 606-10-55-46 through 55-49). Agents increasingly bill for outcomes. Intercom charges 99 cents each time its Fin agent resolves a customer conversation, counts a customer who goes quiet for 24 hours as resolved and deducts the charge if that customer returns, even in a later billing period (Intercom Fin help center). That is revenue that can quietly reverse. And the industry's headline metric, annualized run-rate, is defined by no accounting standard at all. It is a recent month or quarter projected over a year, calculated however each company chooses.

Money also moves in circles. In February, OpenAI announced $110 billion of new investment, including $30 billion from Nvidia and $50 billion from Amazon; in the same announcement it expanded its Amazon Web Services agreement by $100 billion over eight years and agreed to use five gigawatts of Nvidia systems (OpenAI; CNBC, Feb. 27). In August, Nvidia disclosed residual-value guarantees, capped at $105 billion, on leases at an Ohio data-center campus where an OpenAI affiliate is the tenant and will run Nvidia's platform; OpenAI has agreed to reimburse Nvidia for any payments (Nvidia Form 8-K, Aug. 17). Under ASC 606, cash a seller pays its customer generally reduces the seller's revenue (ASC 606-10-32-25). An equity investment at fair value generally does not, and guarantees fall under separate rules. A supplier can therefore invest in a customer, backstop that customer's rent and report its purchases as ordinary sales, with the connections spread across different footnotes. Nothing in that is improper. Almost none of it is visible from the top line.

We have been here before. In the late 1990s, online travel sites reported the full price of tickets they held only briefly; Priceline's revenue for the first nine months of 1999 would have been $255 million lower had it been reported net (The CPA Journal, March 2001). Dot-coms also swapped advertising space and booked both sides as revenue. The Financial Accounting Standards Board's Emerging Issues Task Force did not leave this to firm-by-firm judgment. It limited barter-advertising revenue in January 2000 (Issue 99-17) and in July 2000 reached a consensus on reporting revenue gross or net, with indicators for when a company acts as principal or agent (Issue 99-19). That guidance has since been replaced by today's control test. The lesson is not that the old rules were better. It is that a targeted interpretation, issued within months, settled what general principles could not.

The usual answer today is that public listings will cure this. OpenAI has confidentially filed for an initial public offering and Anthropic is preparing one (CNBC, Oct. 8). An I.P.O. will put the numbers under audit. It will not make them mean the same thing. Two prospectuses, each with a clean audit opinion, can describe the same kind of sale in ways that differ by tens of billions of dollars. And by the time they arrive, the private figures will have done their work. They already sit inside the valuations of chipmakers, cloud providers, utilities and lenders, and behind an investment boom that now shows up in the national accounts: when the Bureau of Economic Analysis revised second-quarter growth upward last month, data centers led the upward revision to business investment in structures (BEA, Sept. 30).

What would help is narrow and achievable. The FASB and the International Accounting Standards Board should jointly issue an A.I.-specific interpretation of how the control test applies when models are sold through cloud marketplaces, how prepaid tokens and their breakage are recognized, and how outcome-based fees are estimated. They should also require a short reconciliation in the revenue note showing sales on a gross basis, on a net basis and by channel, so investors can line up any two companies themselves. Sales to customers in which the seller holds equity, to which it has extended guarantees or from which it buys capacity belong in one disclosure, not scattered across notes. And the Securities and Exchange Commission, which already expects companies to define the metrics they publicize (SEC Release 33-10751, 2020), should require any company citing run-rate revenue in a registration statement to reconcile it to recognized revenue on both bases.

Boards need not wait. Every audit committee whose forecast depends on A.I. demand can ask management one question this quarter: On which basis were the numbers we rely on prepared, and what would they be on the other?

Twenty-six years ago, standard-setters settled gross versus net within months, when the gap at a company like Priceline ran to hundreds of millions of dollars. Today the gap at a single company is $20 billion. We should write the rule before the listings, not after the restatements.

I am a CPA with an MBA, and I work in corporate finance, where revenue recognition, reporting comparability and audit are part of daily work. The essay's argument, that gross-versus-net revenue recognition makes AI companies' numbers incomparable, comes directly from applying these accounting standards in practice.
