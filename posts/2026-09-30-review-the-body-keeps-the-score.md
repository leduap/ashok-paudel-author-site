The Body Keeps the Score: Brain, Mind, and Body in the Healing of Trauma is a psychiatrist's account of what severe stress does to people and how they might recover. Viking published it in September 2014 and the Penguin paperback followed a year later. Few books have stayed on the lists so long: it was number 8 on the New York Times combined print and e-book nonfiction list dated 27 September 2026, in its 312th week.

## The short answer

This is a book about psychological trauma written for general readers by a clinician with decades of practice. It has nothing to do with finance, and it is reviewed here only because it is one of the most durable titles on the nonfiction list. Many readers have found it illuminating and kind. It has also drawn sustained criticism from researchers who say that some of its central scientific claims go beyond the evidence and that it is too cool towards well-tested treatments. The fair summary is that it is an influential and compassionate book whose science should not be taken on trust. This review is shorter than others on the site because fewer details of the book's contents could be checked against independent sources. Nothing here is medical advice.

## Who it is for

- General readers who want to know why this book has shaped the way trauma is talked about.
- People supporting someone with a history of trauma who want a humane, story-led introduction.
- Readers interested in how a popular science book can outrun the research behind it.
- Clinicians and students who expect to meet patients who have read it.

## Who it is not for

- Anyone seeking a guide to choosing treatment. That is a matter for a qualified professional, and the book's preferences are disputed.
- Readers who want settled science set out with its uncertainties. Critics say this is where the book is weakest.
- People who may find detailed accounts of abuse and violence distressing.
- Readers looking for anything on money, work or decision-making in the usual sense.

## How the book is organised

The publisher lists the Penguin paperback at 464 pages. Neither the publisher's page nor the author's website gave a table of contents that could be opened, so this review does not list parts or chapters.

The sources do show what the book is made of. The author's website describes it as the story of therapists and scientists, together with their patients, trying to bring brain science, research on early attachment and awareness of the body into treatment. The publisher describes two movements: first an explanation of how trauma alters body and brain, then a survey of treatments. A review in New Scientist, quoted in the book's Wikipedia entry, calls it dense with both science and case histories and warns that it becomes technical in places. Wikipedia also notes that the book grew from a journal article of the same name that van der Kolk published in 1994.

## Key ideas

### Trauma is common

The publisher's description opens with figures on how many Americans have experienced abuse, grown up with alcoholic relatives or lived with violence at home. The purpose is to move trauma from the edge of medicine, where it was associated mainly with soldiers, to the centre of ordinary life. Whatever a reader concludes about the rest, this widening of attention is the book's most lasting effect.

### The claim in the title

The central argument is that overwhelming experience changes the body and the brain, not only the memory, and that survivors can lose some capacity for pleasure, trust and self-control as a result. That is the publisher's summary of the thesis. It is also the most disputed part. Wikipedia reports a 2025 review in a journal of the Royal College of Psychiatrists which weighed 122 of the book's claims against the evidence. Its finding was that current research does not back what the book says about trauma damaging the brain.

### Relationships can harm and can heal

According to Wikipedia's overview, van der Kolk gives a central place to attachment, meaning the bonds formed with carers in early life, and to a person's social surroundings in protecting against lasting harm. The publisher makes the same point about the power of relationships in both directions. This is the least controversial of the book's themes.

### Many routes to recovery

The second half surveys treatments. The publisher lists neurofeedback, meditation, sport, drama and yoga, and Wikipedia adds a therapy based on guided eye movements known as EMDR. The common idea is that recovery may need to involve the body and not only conversation. Critics accept that some of these approaches have support and object to the book's suggestion that body-based methods are uniquely effective. Wikipedia also states that the book discourages readers from established treatments such as cognitive behavioural therapy, a structured talking therapy with a strong evidence base.

## What works

- **It takes suffering seriously.** The New Scientist review singled out the patients' struggles and resilience as the most affecting part of the book.
- **It widened the conversation.** The 2025 journal review, though critical, accepts that the book met a deep public interest in trauma and shaped what patients and clinicians expect.
- **It is written by a practitioner.** The publisher notes more than three decades of work with survivors, and the case material comes from that experience.
- **It is ambitious.** It tries to connect research on the brain, child development and therapy in one account for a general reader.

## What to be aware of

- **Key scientific claims are contested.** Besides the 2025 review of 122 claims, Wikipedia cites a 2023 journal editorial accusing the book of misrepresenting the research on treatment and of straying towards pseudoscience.
- **Researchers say their work was misdescribed.** A 2024 article in Mother Jones, as summarised by Wikipedia, reported that several scientists cited in the book said their findings had been misread or misreported.
- **It may steer readers from proven help.** The criticism that it undervalues cognitive behavioural therapy matters, because a reader in distress might act on it.
- **A major newspaper has been critical.** The Washington Post published a negative assessment in 2023, describing the science as uncertain.
- **The underlying theory was challenged early.** Wikipedia records that the psychologist Richard McNally criticised the 1994 article on which the book is based for claims that lacked evidence.
- **The author is active in the field he describes.** His website offers workshops and links to a research foundation. That is normal for a clinician, and it means he is an advocate for his approach.
- **It is long and sometimes technical.** At 464 pages it asks for patience.

## How it compares

On this site the nearest comparison is the review of [The Anxious Generation](../2026-09-09-review-the-anxious-generation/). Both are best sellers by scholars, both make a strong claim about cause, both have shaped public discussion, and both have been told by other researchers that the evidence is thinner than the book suggests. [When Breath Becomes Air](../2026-09-22-review-when-breath-becomes-air/) is a very different book about medicine, a memoir with no theory to defend.

There is one point of contact with this site's usual subjects. A book's time on a best-seller list measures how many people bought it and nothing else. That is as true of confident books about markets as it is of this one, and [A Random Walk Down Wall Street](../2026-09-28-review-a-random-walk-down-wall-street/) is a good model of how to test a persuasive claim against data.

## The verdict

An important book in the sense that it changed how a great many people think, and a humane one. It is not a reliable guide to the science, on the evidence of the specialist reviews. Read it for its stories and its sympathy, hold its explanations loosely, and take questions about treatment to a professional.

## FAQ

### Is the science in the book accepted?

Parts of it are disputed. A 2025 review in a psychiatric journal found that current research does not back two of its main claims: that trauma damages the brain, and that treatments working through the body are specially effective.

### Is it a self-help book?

It is closer to popular science mixed with clinical stories. It describes treatments and does not provide a programme to follow.

### Why is it still on the best-seller list?

The sources point to a surge of public interest in trauma and psychology in recent years. The Financial Times, quoted by Wikipedia, called its success improbable for so dense a book.

### How long is it?

The publisher lists 464 pages for the paperback.
