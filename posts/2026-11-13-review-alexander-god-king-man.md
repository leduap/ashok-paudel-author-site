Alexander: God, King, Man is a biography of Alexander of Macedon by Edmund Richardson, a classicist at Durham University. The edition described here is the Bloomsbury hardback of 4 June 2026, a first edition listed at 480 pages. A first US edition from St. Martin's Press followed later in 2026. The publisher's page, and a Guardian review dated 4 November 2026, both call the book the winner of that year's Baillie Gifford Prize.

## The short answer

A life told as a story. Richardson has new archaeology and, according to the Guardian, material from twelve languages behind him, and he spends it on pace, concrete battles and a running argument about how a young king was turned into a god. The Irish Times says he also prints the warning every honest Alexander book needs: the colourful tales were written at least three centuries after the death. Read it if you want the campaign to feel close, and if you can keep that warning in mind while it does. It is not a manual of tactics, and it is not a calm weighing of every earlier biography.

## Who it is for

- Readers who want one modern narrative of Alexander and do not need a footnote on every page.
- Readers who can take a violent book when the violence is the subject, not a thrill added on.
- Anyone interested in how a reputation is built: king, hero, living god, then a death that does not match the legend.
- Readers of the narrative histories already on this site who want an ancient life told in a similar spirit.

## Who it is not for

- Readers who want a military textbook, with supply lines and orders of battle set out as tables.
- Readers who want older biographers quoted, answered and filed.
- Anyone looking for a hero with the mass killing left out.
- Readers who will only trust a scene if a witness wrote it down at the time.

## How the book is organised

A US library record for the St. Martin's edition prints the contents in full. This review uses that list for the Bloomsbury book as well. A separate UK contents page was not opened, so a chapter may fall on a different page, but the titles run in the order of the life.

There are twenty-four chapters and an epilogue. They open with The Watchers and The Battle, then The Boy from the Hills, The Throne and The Knot. The long middle is the war and the remaking of the king: The Engineer, Blood, The Weight of a Heart, The City, Man to God, The Choice, Dust, Earth and Heaven, The Golden City, Nemesis and The Great King. The last movement goes out to the edge of the known world and then comes apart: World's End, The Kiss, Legends, The Elephant, Mutiny, The Desert, The City of Ashes and Starlight. The epilogue is The Matter of the Body.

A chapter title says what the chapter is about. It does not say what Richardson argues. The scenes below come from reviews that describe particular passages, not from a reading of each chapter in the book.

Bloomsbury lists the UK hardback at 480 pages. The US library record counts 469 pages plus eight pages of plates, with maps, a bibliography and an index. Same life, different counting.

## Key ideas

### A disputed throne, not a ready-made legend

The Guardian's review starts in 336 BC. Alexander is twenty. His father, Philip, has been assassinated, and the young man is king of Macedon without having looked, the review says, like the person anyone was waiting for. He then marches against Darius III, ruler of a far larger empire, who is confident enough to bring his family to watch. The review's picture of the eve of that first great battle is deliberately unheroic: a fever, argument in the ranks, and an enemy who expects to win. The legend, on this telling, begins as a gamble by someone who could easily have lost the throne in a season.

### Each battle is given a machine

The Guardian says the book goes from fight to fight, and that Richardson keeps each one specific. At Issus the device is the phalanx: a deep block of men with very long spears. At the siege of Tyre the device is engineering, catapults and machines that lift people and smash them on the rocks. At Gaugamela the review says he notes a practical mistake on the Persian side, horses that would not face the smell of elephants. The Irish Times praises that physical writing, the burning of Thebes included. The praise is also a warning. The book does not stand back from slaughter.

### The god, and the mother who refuses the title

On the road to the oasis at Siwa, the Guardian says, Alexander accepts the Egyptian name of son of the god Amun. The review quotes a letter in which he greets his mother, Olympias, under that divine title, and gives her answer as a mother's rebuke: do not call yourself that. In Susa the same review describes a drinking bout in which he promises to clear his soldiers' debts and pay for their marriages. The reviewer reports Richardson's name for it, "the most expensive hangover in history". Taken together, the two scenes are the book's double question. The divine claim and the political bill arrive at the same time, and neither is secure. The Guardian says the book keeps asking which office he actually holds, and whose son he is willing to be.

### What outlasted the army

One chapter, the Guardian says, is about Alexandria and its library, a subject of Richardson's 2021 book on the search for that city. The review lists an early steam-engine design, a close measure of the earth's size, mechanical birds and standard texts of Homer, and says Richardson ranks that legacy above most of the conquest. A reader tired of battles can treat the chapter as the book's argument about what lasted. It is also a very large claim.

### A small death, and late stories

The Guardian says he dies at thirty-two in Babylon, grieving his closest friend, drinking hard, and afraid. The review gives the likely cause as a typhoid infection from river water, an ordinary end after so many near-misses, and it quotes the book's own fall: boy, king, hero, living god, then a broken death. The Irish Times adds the disclaimer. Richardson tells the reader that the bright anecdotes were written at least three hundred years later, and then tells them anyway: the horse, the cutting of the Gordian knot, Diogenes telling the king to get out of his sun. The book wants both the rush and the caution. Whether a reader can hold both is the test of the book.

## What works

- **It is organised as one life.** The contents run from a boy and a throne to a body in Babylon, which is the right order for a biography.
- **The case for a new book is the research, not the fame of the subject.** The Guardian's reason is the range of languages and the newer finds, not the fact that Alexander is already famous.
- **The tone can be funny without dropping the scholarship.** The Guardian mentions asides that puncture Greek contempt for Persians. The comedy is how the book stays readable.
- **It has a chapter for what survived the wars.** The library, as that review describes it, is the counterweight to the battles.
- **The prize is a reported fact about the book.** Both the publisher and the Guardian call it the 2026 Baillie Gifford winner. The Guardian also reports the prize money as £50,000. That is a verdict on the writing, not on Alexander.

## What to be aware of

- **The lively scenes are late.** The Irish Times says the book itself puts the colourful stories at least three centuries after the death. A scene that feels witnessed was not witnessed by the author, or by anyone whose account we have from the time.
- **The largest sentence is a boast.** The Guardian quotes Richardson to the effect that Alexander reshaped reality more deeply than almost anyone, from the stars overhead to the words on the page. The Irish Times calls a similar line grandiose. You can like the book and still think that sentence is there to win the reader, not to settle a comparison with other historical figures.
- **The violence is the book, not a passage you can skip.** Conquest, destroyed cities and a paranoid court are the plot. The Irish Times also thinks the attempt to diagnose his character across that distance is guesswork.
- **The reviews describe a style and a synthesis, not a single find that scraps the older biographies.** If you want a chapter that lines this account up against the standard modern lives and says where each one is wrong, look elsewhere.
- **The two English hardbacks are not the same object.** A line will not sit on the same page in both, and this review has not compared the texts word for word.
- **Some of the scholarly argument is happening off to the side.** The pace is a choice. A general reader will not see every fight with earlier historians.

## How it compares

This site has no other ancient biography, so the useful comparisons are books that make a large story feel near. [1929](../2026-09-11-review-1929/) is a character-led history of a financial crash, stronger on people and mood than on the economics of why the crash became a depression. Richardson makes the same trade: the scene first, the apparatus second. [London Falling](../2026-09-18-review-london-falling/) is careful reported narrative, and it cannot close its case. Richardson can close his scene, the death in Babylon, and still cannot close the man. The Irish Times quotes his own line that there will never be a definitive Alexander. That is the sentence to put next to both of those books.

On which copy to get: the Bloomsbury hardback is the first edition, and it is the one the Guardian reviewed at £25. The St. Martin's edition is the same life in the American format, with plates. The publisher also lists a paperback. This review has not compared the texts word for word.

## The verdict

The Alexander book to read if you want the wars, the self-invention and a joke in the same stretch of prose, and if you can remember that the man died more than two thousand years before the best-known stories about him were written. It earns its speed. It does not earn a claim to be the last word.

## FAQ

### Who wrote it, and is it by Alexander?

It is a modern biography. Edmund Richardson teaches classics at Durham. The publisher's contributor note calls him an associate professor. Alexander did not write it.

### Which edition is this review describing?

The Bloomsbury hardback published on 4 June 2026, listed at 480 pages, ISBN 9781526658173. The first US edition is from St. Martin's Press, ISBN 9781250285614. A library catalogue counts that edition as 469 pages plus eight pages of plates.

### Did it win the Baillie Gifford Prize?

The publisher's page says it did, and the Guardian's review of 4 November 2026 reports the win and a prize of £50,000. This review did not open the prize's own site.

### Can the famous anecdotes be trusted?

The Irish Times says Richardson includes the standard warning: those stories were written at least three hundred years after the death. The horse, the knot and the meeting with Diogenes are in the book, told with that warning attached.
