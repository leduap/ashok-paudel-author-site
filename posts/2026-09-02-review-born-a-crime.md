Born a Crime: Stories from a South African Childhood is Trevor Noah's memoir of his childhood and youth. It was first published by Spiegel and Grau in November 2016; the paperback from One World, the edition on current best-seller lists, followed in February 2019. Almost ten years after it first appeared it is still selling: it stood at number 11 on the New York Times combined print and e-book nonfiction list dated 6 September 2026, its 103rd week on that list.

## The short answer

This is a memoir, told as a set of stories, about a mixed-race boy raised by a determined mother while South Africa moved from apartheid to democracy. Most readers come for the humour and the portrait of his mother. A finance-minded reader will also find something less expected: a ground-level description of how a lack of money, knowledge and safety nets holds people where they are, and several small business ventures described with real candour. It is not a money book and offers no method. It also contains violence, including violence at home, and at least one major reviewer found the jokes and the grim material an uneasy mix.

## Who it is for

- Readers who want a personal account of the end of apartheid from someone who was a child at the time.
- Anyone interested in how people earn a living where formal jobs are scarce, told by someone who did it.
- Readers who like memoirs built from separate episodes that can be read one at a time.
- Parents and teachers looking for a book that older teenagers will read willingly.

## Who it is not for

- Readers looking for the story of Noah's comedy and television career. The book stops before it.
- Anyone wanting a history of South Africa. The background is supplied in short passages, not at length.
- Readers who prefer one continuous narrative. Kirkus called the result somewhat disjointed.
- Readers who would find descriptions of domestic violence and poverty too distressing.

## How the book is organised

The publisher lists the One World paperback at 336 pages; the original hardcover is recorded at 304. The study guide site LitCharts, which summarises the book chapter by chapter, shows eighteen chapters in three parts. Chapters 1 to 8 cover early childhood and family. Chapters 9 to 14 are shorter stories from adolescence: school, friendships and early attempts at romance. Chapters 15 to 18 follow him towards independence at the end of high school and after it, and end with the most serious events in the book.

Kirkus describes the chapters as loosely connected essays that start at home and school and then move outward to different neighbourhoods. The stories are not in strict date order. This review is written from the publisher's details, published reviews and the chapter summaries listed under Sources.

## Key ideas

### A birth that broke the law

The title is literal. Noah's father was a white man of Swiss-German background and his mother a black Xhosa woman, and at the time he was born a relationship between them was a criminal offence. The publisher says he spent much of his earliest years indoors, hidden from the authorities. Under the racial categories of the time he fitted nowhere neatly, and much of the book is about learning to move between groups. Wikipedia's summary notes that he used his command of several languages to be accepted where his appearance marked him as an outsider.

### The mother at the centre

By every account the main character is his mother, Patricia. The publisher calls her fearless, rebellious and deeply religious. Kirkus stresses that she chose to have and raise a child across the colour line with no reason to expect apartheid to end, and prepared him for a freedom that did not yet exist. Her method combined strict discipline, constant church attendance and an insistence on education. The book reads partly as an extended thank-you.

### Poverty that was designed, and then persisted

Here is where the book speaks most directly to this site's subjects. According to the LitCharts analysis, Noah presents the poverty around him as intended by the old system, which restricted where black South Africans could live and what they could learn. His sharper observation is that removing the laws did not remove the result. People were free to leave poor neighbourhoods and could not afford to. Older relatives had been denied the schooling and the inherited know-how about money that wealthier families pass on without noticing.

Two examples from the same analysis make the point concrete. When a white family's house burned down, insurance covered the loss; when his own mother needed hospital care, there was no insurance and the bill fell on him. And when his stepfather bought a car repair business, the family did not understand that they were taking on its debts as well, and it ruined them.

### Small business with no cushion

As a teenager Noah resold food from the school canteen, then built what he calls a small empire copying and selling music CDs, then worked as a DJ and as a middleman buying and reselling goods in the township of Alexandra. The copied CDs and many of the goods were not legitimate, by the book's own account as the summaries report it. What a finance reader will notice is the fragility. LitCharts records that the trade earned too little to save from or to pay for university, and that when police destroyed his computer during an event, the business was finished. One piece of equipment was the whole enterprise.

### The "black tax"

Noah and his mother use this phrase, according to LitCharts, for the obligation on anyone who gets ahead to support the relatives who have not. Income that might have become savings goes to lifting the family first. It is a useful idea for anyone who assumes that two people on the same salary have the same capacity to build wealth.

## What works

- **It shows economics at street level.** Insurance, debt, working capital and schooling appear as things that happen to named people, which makes them easier to grasp than any definition.
- **It is frank about the ventures.** On the summaries' evidence, the illegal side of the trading is stated openly, and so is the fact that it led nowhere.
- **The episodes stand alone.** A reader can take one chapter at a sitting.
- **The central portrait is strong.** Reviewers consistently single out the mother as the reason the book stays with people.
- **It reaches readers who avoid serious books.** The humour carries a good deal of history and social observation that might otherwise go unread.

## What to be aware of

- **The structure is loose.** Kirkus judged the narrative somewhat disjointed and the material not fully worked through.
- **The tone can jar.** The same review found that the attempt at comedy often sits awkwardly beside what is being described.
- **It is one person's memory.** A memoir is not a documented history, and the generalisations about South Africa are a comedian's, not a scholar's.
- **It ends early.** Readers hoping to learn how he built a career abroad will not find it here.
- **There is no advice in it.** The lessons about money are there to be drawn by the reader. Nothing is set out as guidance, and the circumstances are far from those of most readers of this site.
- **Some content is violent.** The last part includes a shooting within the family.

## How it compares

Among the reviews on this site, [The Psychology of Money](../2026-09-14-review-the-psychology-of-money/) argues in general terms that people's financial behaviour is shaped by where and when they grew up. Born a Crime is one detailed example of that claim. [Buy Then Build](../2026-11-02-review-buy-then-build/) explains how to buy an existing small business, including what comes with it; the stepfather's repair shop in this book is the cautionary version of that purchase. [Your Money or Your Life](../2026-10-12-review-your-money-or-your-life/) and similar books assume a reader with a surplus to manage. This memoir is a reminder of how much has to be in place before that advice applies.

On editions, the first hardcover came from Spiegel and Grau in 2016. The One World paperback of 2019 is the one now on the lists.

## The verdict

Read it as a memoir first: a vivid, often funny account of an unusual childhood and a remarkable parent. Its value for a reader interested in money is a side effect, but a real one. Few books show as plainly why starting without savings, insurance or financial knowledge is so hard to escape.

## FAQ

### Is this a book about money?

No. It is a memoir of childhood and adolescence. Money, debt and small trading run through it because they ran through the author's early life.

### Does it cover Trevor Noah's time on television?

No. The stories end in his early adulthood in South Africa.

### Is it suitable for teenagers?

Its Wikipedia entry records that it has been set as required reading on a college English course. It does contain violence and adult themes, so a parent may want to look at it first.

### Which edition is on the best-seller list?

The One World paperback, published in February 2019. The publisher lists it at 336 pages.
