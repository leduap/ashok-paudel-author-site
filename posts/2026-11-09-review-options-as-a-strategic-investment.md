Options as a Strategic Investment was first published in 1980. The fifth edition, reviewed here, came out in August 2012 and runs to 1,072 pages. Its author, Lawrence G. McMillan, is a professional trader and the founder of McMillan Analysis Corporation. The book is often called the reference work on its subject.

## The short answer

A reference book, and a very complete one. Each strategy gets its own chapter explaining how it works, when it suits and what to do as the position changes. It is not for beginners: the publisher says it is written for investors who already have some familiarity with the options market. It is long, dense and dated in places, and a book this thorough can make risky positions look more manageable than they are. Own it if you already trade options and want one dependable book to check your thinking against; do not start here.

## Who it is for

- Readers who already understand calls and puts and want one dependable book to check their thinking against.
- People studying for work in options or derivatives.
- Traders who use a few strategies and want the full treatment of each.

## Who it is not for

- Beginners. Start with something much shorter.
- Readers who want a book to read from cover to cover.
- Anyone looking for a simple route to income. Options can lose money quickly, and some strategies described can lose more than the amount put in.
- Long-term investors who hold index funds and have no particular reason to add options.

## How the book is organised

The table of contents on the author's site lists 43 chapters in six parts.

- **Part I: basic properties of stock options.** One chapter of definitions: what influences an option's price, how exercise and assignment work, and how orders are entered.
- **Part II: call option strategies.** Thirteen chapters, beginning with covered call writing and running through call buying, naked call writing and a long series of spreads.
- **Part III: put option strategies.** Put buying, puts held alongside shares, the sale of a put, straddles, synthetic positions, put spreads and long-dated options known as LEAPS.
- **Part IV: additional considerations.** Options with Treasury bills, arbitrage and mathematical applications.
- **Part V: index options and futures.** Including hedging a share portfolio with index products, and a chapter on structured products.
- **Part VI: measuring and trading volatility.** Six chapters, followed by a chapter on taxes and a closing chapter titled "The Best Strategy?".

The contents list gives the headings inside each chapter, and that is what the notes below rely on. It shows what McMillan covers and in what order. The plain-English explanations of the terms are mine, and the numbers are illustrations, not examples from the book.

## Key ideas

### Start with the conservative strategy

An option is a contract giving its buyer the right to buy (a call) or sell (a put) a share at a fixed price before a set date. The seller, or "writer", is paid a premium for taking on the obligation.

It says something that the first strategy in the book is covered call writing: owning shares and selling calls against them. The chapter's headings include "The Importance of Covered Call Writing", "Covered Writing Philosophy", "The Total Return Concept of Covered Writing" and "Computing Return on Investment". The ordering puts the strategy that starts from share ownership ahead of outright speculation.

*An illustration with round numbers, not taken from the book:* you own 100 shares at 50 and sell a call with a strike price of 55 for a premium of 2 a share, receiving 200. If the shares rise to 70, you must sell at 55: you keep 7 a share and give up the other 13. If they fall to 30, you lose 20 a share less the 2 received. The premium is small; the share risk is still all yours.

### Follow-up action

The most distinctive feature is in the headings: chapter after chapter has a section called "Follow-Up Action". Covered writes, call purchases, spreads, put sales and straddles each get one. This is the question shorter books skip: the position is on, the share price has moved, what now? Options run out on a fixed date, so doing nothing is itself a decision.

### Selling options without cover

The book treats uncovered, or "naked", selling openly, with sections titled "The Philosophy of Selling Naked Options" and "Risk and Reward". The chapter on the sale of a put includes "Evaluating a Naked Put Write" and "Buying Stock below Its Market Price", the idea that a put seller is in effect agreeing to buy shares at a lower price and being paid to wait.

*An illustration with round numbers, not taken from the book:* you sell a put with a strike price of 45 on 100 shares and receive 150. If the shares fall to 30, you must buy at 45 what is worth 30: a loss of 1,500 against the 150 received.

### Protection as well as income

Options are also insurance. One chapter covers buying puts alongside shares you own, including "Put Buying As Protection for the Covered Call Writer" and "No-Cost Collars", where the cost of a protective put is met by selling a call. Part V extends this to whole portfolios, and the author's site says the fifth edition explains portfolio protection using index puts or the newer volatility call options.

### Volatility as the real subject

Part VI moves from the direction of prices to how much they move. Its headings include "Implied Volatility", "The Volatility of Volatility", "Why Does Volatility Reach Extremes?", "Misconceptions about Volatility" and "Two Ways Volatility Predictions Can Be Wrong". Implied volatility is the amount of future movement the current option price assumes. A chapter on advanced concepts covers the "Greeks", the standard measures of how an option's price responds to changes in the share price, time and volatility. According to the author's site, the main addition in the fifth edition is a full discussion of volatility futures and options.

### The unglamorous chapters

There are full chapters on mathematics, on arbitrage and on taxes, and the publisher highlights the detailed examples, exhibits and checklists. These are what make it a working reference instead of a book of ideas.

## What works

- **It is complete.** Almost any standard strategy has a chapter.
- **It deals with management, not only entry.** The follow-up sections are where many shorter books stop.
- **It is organised for looking things up.** A reader can go straight to the strategy in question.
- **It takes risk and reward strategy by strategy,** with sections on the investment required and on evaluation.
- **A study guide is sold alongside it,** with reading assignments and quizzes, for those who want to work through it.

## What to be aware of

- **It is not for beginners.** The publisher says so, and one chapter of definitions at the start is not enough preparation for the other 42.
- **It is very long.** Few readers will finish it, and it is not designed for that.
- **The edition dates from 2012.** Markets, products and trading costs have moved on. The examples reflect the commission rates and products of that time, and the tax chapter describes United States rules as they stood then.
- **Completeness can mislead.** Forty-odd chapters of strategies, each with its follow-up action, can leave the impression that every position can be managed out of trouble. Uncovered positions in particular can produce losses far larger than the income received, and understanding a strategy on paper is not the same as being able to carry its losses.
- **It is written for readers who have already decided to trade options.** The case that most investors are better served by simply holding the market is made elsewhere.
- **The author sells related services** through his own company, as described on its website.

## How it compares

This is the only specialist trading book on the site, and it sits at the opposite end from the index-fund books. [A Random Walk Down Wall Street](../2026-09-28-review-a-random-walk-down-wall-street/) and [The Little Book of Common Sense Investing](../2026-09-21-review-the-little-book-of-common-sense-investing/) both argue that activity and cost reduce returns for investors as a group; anyone considering options should read one of them first and decide with that argument in mind. [The Intelligent Investor](../2026-10-05-review-the-intelligent-investor/) draws a line between investment and speculation that is worth holding up against each strategy here.

On editions: the fifth is current, and the author's site highlights what is new in it: the volatility material, portfolio protection, and examples updated for current symbols, lower commissions and weekly options.

## The verdict

The book to own if you are serious about options and already know the basics. Read the chapters on the strategies you use, including their follow-up sections, and keep the rest for when you need it. If you are still deciding whether options belong in your investing at all, this is the wrong first book.

## FAQ

### Is Options as a Strategic Investment suitable for beginners?

No. The publisher describes it as written for investors who have some familiarity with the options market.

### Which edition is current?

The fifth edition, published in August 2012. It is the one linked on this page.

### Is there a study guide?

Yes. The author's site sells a separate study guide with reading assignments and quizzes for each chapter.

### What is a covered call?

Owning shares and selling someone the right to buy them at a set price by a set date, in return for a payment. It adds some income and caps the gain.
