The Serviceberry: Abundance and Reciprocity in the Natural World is a small book about a large question: what an economy is for. Robin Wall Kimmerer, a botanist, uses a fruiting shrub to argue that sharing and exchange without price deserve a place beside buying and selling. Scribner published it on 19 November 2024, with line drawings by John Burgoyne. Nearly two years later it was number 5 on the New York Times hardcover nonfiction list dated 13 September 2026, in its 33rd week there.

## The short answer

This is a reflective essay in book form, not a study of economics. It suits a reader who wants to step back from prices and returns and think about what money cannot measure: goodwill, obligation, the things neighbours do for one another. It is brief, warm and easy to finish in one sitting. Its limits follow from the same qualities. It does not test its ideas against the hard cases, such as people who take without giving back, or how sharing works among millions of strangers. Reviewers have also said that it is gentle about the conflicts a real change in the economy would involve.

## Who it is for

- Readers who spend their working lives with budgets and prices and want a different angle on what counts as wealth.
- People interested in community projects such as tool libraries, food sharing or free book exchanges.
- Readers of Kimmerer's earlier Braiding Sweetgrass who want a shorter, more focused companion.
- Anyone looking for a thoughtful gift book that can be read in an hour or two.

## Who it is not for

- Readers who want economic argument with data, models or evidence on both sides.
- People looking for personal finance guidance. There is none.
- Readers who dislike a lyrical, earnest style. Kirkus, in a favourable review, still says the prose sometimes tips towards the sugary.
- Anyone expecting a detailed plan for changing economic policy.

## How the book is organised

Kirkus Reviews lists the Scribner hardcover at 112 pages. The Guardian describes it as a short book that grew out of a magazine essay, with simple drawings throughout. None of the sources consulted gives a chapter list, so this review does not describe one.

The reviews do show how the argument moves. It starts with the author picking serviceberries on a neighbour's farm while birds feed on the same bushes. From that scene she widens out: first to how the plant's fruit comes to exist and where it goes, then to human societies that organise themselves around giving, then to modern examples closer to home, and lastly to whether such arrangements can live beside the market. Along the way, according to the magazine Northern Woodlands, she brings in conversations with economists as well as biology and stories from her own Potawatomi heritage.

This review is written from the publisher's details and the published reviews listed under Sources.

## Key ideas

### A berry bush as an economy

Kimmerer's opening move is to ask whether what happens around the shrub is an economy at all. Leaves feed the soil, insects pollinate the flowers, birds carry the seeds, and the fruit feeds whoever comes. Goods move and needs are met, yet nothing is priced and nothing is owed in the usual sense. The Northern Woodlands review adds a detail from the book: the Potawatomi name for the plant contains a root meaning gift. The point is not that nature is kind. It is that distribution without a market is ordinary, and all around us.

### Wealth as having enough to share

In a market, value rises with scarcity. In what Kimmerer calls a gift economy, the reviews explain, standing comes from what a person gives away, and security comes from the relationships that giving creates. Her recurring example, as reported by Northern Woodlands, is a hunter who shares a large kill with his whole community instead of keeping it, on the reasoning that well-fed neighbours are a better store than a private stockpile. For a finance reader this is a familiar idea in unfamiliar clothes: it treats goodwill as an asset and mutual help as a kind of insurance.

### Enough is a decision

Kirkus highlights the book's notion of "enoughness": the claim that deciding one has sufficient is a deliberate act in a culture that keeps urging more. This connects directly to personal money choices. Much household stress comes not from a shortage of income but from a target that moves upward each time it is reached. Kimmerer comes at that from ecology and tradition where a finance writer would come at it from savings rates, but the conclusion is close.

### Gift economies already exist beside the market

The book does not ask readers to imagine something exotic. According to the Society of Environmental Journalists' review, it points to free neighbourhood book boxes, roadside stands of surplus vegetables, open-source software and Wikipedia. It also counts public libraries and parks, which are paid for through taxes but shared without a price at the door. The same review reports that Kimmerer questions the well-known "tragedy of the commons" argument, which holds that people will always overuse what they hold in common, by pointing to communities that manage shared resources with agreed rules.

### Alongside, not instead

Kimmerer does not predict the end of markets. The Society of Environmental Journalists' reviewer quotes her as saying she does not expect market capitalism to vanish, and that the realistic aim is to encourage gift arrangements that run next to it. That modesty is important for judging the book fairly. It is an invitation to notice and extend what already works at the scale of a street or a town, and a criticism of an economy organised only around accumulation.

## What works

- **It makes an abstract idea concrete.** Starting from a bucket of fruit gives the reader something to picture, and the modern examples are ones most people have used.
- **It is short and clear.** At about a hundred pages there is no padding, and the argument can be held in the head in one go.
- **It widens the definition of wealth.** Reminding readers that trust and obligation have real economic value is a useful correction to thinking only in money terms.
- **It joins two kinds of knowledge.** The author is a university scientist who belongs to the Citizen Potawatomi Nation, and reviewers credit her with drawing on both without strain.
- **It is realistic about its own reach.** It asks for small local steps and admits that most people remain tied into the market.

## What to be aware of

- **It is not economics.** The reviews describe reflection, stories and examples. There is no sign of data or of engagement with the standard objections, so it should not be read as a tested proposal.
- **The hard problems of scale are left open.** Sharing works well where people know each other. By the reviewers' accounts, the book shows gift arrangements working at the level of a community and does not explain how they would work across a whole country of strangers.
- **It is gentle about conflict.** The Guardian notes that the book's world is mostly one of kind neighbours and free farm stands, with little on the suffering caused by the systems it criticises or the upheaval of replacing them.
- **Some critics want more.** The Guardian also reports that certain Indigenous scholars regard Kimmerer's popular writing as not radical enough and as making Indigenous culture too comfortable for a general audience.
- **The setting is North American.** The plant, the foods and the history it refers to are specific to that continent, though the themes travel.
- **The price per page is high.** The Society of Environmental Journalists lists it at 20 dollars for a book that began as an essay.

## How it compares

The Guardian traces the idea of a gift economy to Lewis Hyde's The Gift (1983), which is the fuller treatment. Kimmerer's own Braiding Sweetgrass (2013) is far longer and ranges across many plants and teachings; The Serviceberry takes one thread from it and follows it into economics. Readers in the United Kingdom should know that the Guardian reviewed an Allen Lane edition with a different subtitle, An Economy of Gifts and Abundance.

On this site, the closest relative is [Your Money or Your Life](../2026-10-12-review-your-money-or-your-life/), which also asks readers to decide what enough means, and then gives a method for acting on the answer. [The Psychology of Money](../2026-09-14-review-the-psychology-of-money/) makes a related case about contentment from inside the world of investing. The Serviceberry is the least practical of the three and the most willing to question the system itself.

## The verdict

A graceful, very short book that asks a finance-minded reader to count things that do not appear on a statement. It is worth an evening for the change of perspective. It should be read as an essay that raises questions, and not as an economic programme that answers them.

## FAQ

### Is this a book about investing or personal finance?

No. It is about how goods and goodwill move between people and through nature. Any lesson for personal money is indirect: mainly the idea of deciding what is enough.

### Does the author want to abolish markets?

Not according to the reviews. She is reported as expecting the market economy to continue and arguing for gift arrangements to grow beside it.

### How long is it?

Kirkus Reviews lists 112 pages for the Scribner hardcover, including illustrations.

### Do I need to read Braiding Sweetgrass first?

No. The reviews treat The Serviceberry as able to stand alone.
