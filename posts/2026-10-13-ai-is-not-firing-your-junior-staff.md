The most important number about artificial intelligence and work this autumn is not a lay-off count. It is a hiring rate. Revelio Labs, which tracks millions of worker profiles, [finds](https://www.reveliolabs.com/charts/after-a-decade-of-growth-global-call-center-employment-is-shrinking/fig-2) that global call-centre employment is 5.3 per cent below its December 2023 peak. Lay-offs explain little of that. In 12 middle-income countries, new hires per 100 call-centre workers fell from 25.1 to 17.2 after November 2022. Nobody was marched out of the building. The door simply stopped opening.

The World Bank sees the same pattern in 285mn US job postings. For occupations most open to substitution by generative AI, [postings fell](https://openknowledge.worldbank.org/entities/publication/b5d5c33c-9419-4dc7-93fb-ec9d6b997c4b) 18 per cent relative to others by the third year after ChatGPT's launch. The drop was 20 per cent for roles that ask for little experience, and 40 per cent in administrative support. A [joint ILO-World Bank paper](https://www.ilo.org/sites/default/files/2026-03/wp166_web.pdf) warns of a "white-collar bypass" in poorer countries: the office jobs that once lifted young people and women into the middle class may never fully appear.

Most commentary treats this as a social problem to be solved by governments: retraining schemes, work-experience pledges, safety nets. That framing lets business off too lightly. The vanishing junior job is first a problem for the companies doing the vanishing.

Almost every senior colleague I speak to learnt the job the same way: by doing dull, high-volume work badly, then less badly, under someone who checked it. Reconciling accounts nobody else wanted. Building the first draft of a forecast that a manager tore apart. That repetition was not waste. It was the training. It is where people learnt what a wrong number feels like before they could prove it was wrong.

AI now does much of that first-draft work well. So the junior role looks redundant, and not replacing a leaver feels like the easiest saving in the budget. No one is fired, no headline is written and the cost line falls.

Here is the part only an accountant tends to notice. Under the accounting standard most of the world uses, money spent on training [must be expensed](https://www.ifrs.org/issued-standards/list-of-standards/ias-38-intangible-assets/) as it is incurred; it can never sit on the balance sheet as an asset. A server can be capitalised. The 25-year-old who will run your treasury in 2040 cannot. So the books show a junior only as a cost, and a cancelled hire only as a gain. Every quarter the arithmetic quietly votes for closing the door.

The bill arrives later, and it is large. A company that stops hiring at the bottom in 2026 will discover around 2032 that it has no one ready to promote into roles that require judgement: the controller who smells a problem in a reconciliation, the engineer who knows when a supplier is bluffing. It will then hire those people from rivals that kept training, at a premium. Anyone who has seen a five-year hiring freeze work its way up an organisation chart knows the pattern. AI will make the gap wider, because it hollows out exactly the years in which experience used to accumulate.

The answer is not to protect yesterday's entry jobs. Nobody needs to defend the right to type invoice numbers. It is to redesign them. If AI does the first draft, the junior's job becomes checking it, explaining it and owning the exception that the model cannot handle. That is harder and more interesting work than the old version, and it gets people to real judgement years sooner. In my experience, a well-supported analyst armed with today's tools can do in their second year work that used to take five. The firms that win the next decade will be the ones that hire juniors on purpose and give them that work, rather than letting attrition decide for them.

Three practical steps would help. Boards should ask management for one number they rarely see: how many people the company hired at entry level this year, compared with five years ago. Finance chiefs should report spending on early-career development separately, so the cost of a pipeline is visible rather than buried in overheads. And managers should treat a junior who checks AI output as an apprentice, not as a cheaper version of the machine.

The ladder into professional life was never a law of nature. Companies built it because it paid. It still does. The only difference now is that it has to be built deliberately.
