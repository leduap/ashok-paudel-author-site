In December 2014, Michigan's unemployment agency told Grant Bauserman, who had drawn jobless benefits, that he had collected money he was not entitled to and had misled the state to get it. With penalties and interest, he owed $19,910. He protested, and in June 2015, with the protest unresolved, the agency seized his tax refund. Months later it reviewed his paperwork, concluded he had been eligible all along and had concealed nothing, declared its own findings "null and void" and returned the money.

Bauserman was not an outlier. From October 2013, Michigan ran fraud detection through an automated system called MiDAS. When the agency later reviewed 22,427 cases from the next two years in which the computer had found fraud, about 93 percent were wrong. The state reversed tens of thousands of fraud findings and refunded about $21 million, and his class action ended in a $20 million settlement, approved in 2024.

The pattern is not only American. Britain's Post Office prosecuted about 700 of its own branch operators between 1999 and 2015, largely on the word of a faulty accounting system called Horizon and helped by a legal presumption that computers work properly unless shown otherwise. Parliament quashed the convictions wholesale in 2024, and more than 600 people have been cleared. Australia's "robodebt" scheme, piloted in 2015 and automated nationwide from 2016, averaged people's annual income and billed them when the average did not match what they had reported; a royal commission called it "a crude and cruel mechanism, neither fair nor legal." In the Netherlands, a self-learning risk model that used Dutch nationality as an indicator helped pick which parents' childcare benefits to scrutinize, and the cabinet resigned.

Few of these systems would count as artificial intelligence today. Most were simple tools that did enormous damage because institutions treated their output as a finding rather than a question. The tools now spreading through American public life are far more convincing: models that flag benefit claims as fraud, decide who keeps health coverage, screen job applicants and freeze bank accounts. Each arrives with a confidence that invites the person reading it to stop thinking.

Most of these decisions never reach a courtroom. They happen in a back office, where a claim is denied or a benefit suspended, and nobody tells the person that a model made the call. Courtroom rules are unsettled too. The federal judiciary's evidence-rules committee published a proposed rule last year holding machine-generated evidence to the reliability standard for expert testimony. In May it declined to recommend the rule and sent a revised version back for further study. No version has been adopted.

My own work offers a simpler test. In financial controlling, an unexplained variance between two systems is the start of an investigation, never the end of one. No competent auditor writes off a balance because a reconciliation tool flagged it; she goes and finds the invoice. The tool tells her where to look. It does not tell her what happened.

That habit should become a rule for any institution that uses automated systems against individuals: a machine's output can open a case, but it can never close one. Before anyone is charged, cut off, billed or arrested, a person must check independent evidence and stand behind it, and the person affected must be told a machine was involved. Above all, the burden of proof belongs to whoever relies on the machine to show that it works, not to the citizen left to prove that it doesn't.

Some of this is happening piecemeal. Michigan has since required employees to review fraud determinations before they are issued. Detroit agreed in a 2024 settlement that its police may not arrest anyone on a facial-recognition match alone. Those instincts are right, but they should not depend on one state's scandal or one city settling one lawsuit. Legislatures and agencies can write the same principle for benefits, credit and hiring before the next scandal.

The objection is cost: checking every flag by hand is slow. But the rule does not ban the machines. It lets them do what they are good at, sorting enormous piles so humans can look in the right place, and stops them doing what they are bad at, which is being right about a particular person. Michigan, Britain and Australia all learned that the bill for skipping that step arrives later, in refunds, settlements and inquiries.

William Blackstone held that it is better that ten guilty persons escape than that one innocent suffer. His ratio was a choice about where errors should fall. Automated suspicion quietly reverses it, because even a system that is right most of the time produces a steady stream of wrongly accused people, at scale and without a face. Let the computer point, and make a human prove it.
