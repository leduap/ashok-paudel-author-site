Britain's government announced on Oct. 28 that it will abolish the standing charge on household gas bills, a fixed fee of about £108 a year that 23 million homes pay just to stay connected, and fold that cost into the price of the gas people actually burn. It sounds like housekeeping.

It is also an early look at a problem America has barely begun to discuss: what happens to the people who are last on a network everyone else is leaving. The country had 73.7 million residential gas customers in 2024, and the number is still edging up.

A utility network is a club with mostly fixed dues. The pipes under a street, the meters, the crews on call and the debt that paid for all of it cost roughly the same whether a hundred homes are connected or ten. When a household leaves, its share of those costs does not vanish. It is handed to the neighbors who stay.

In my job, spreading fixed overhead across what a business produces is routine, and every controller knows the trap. When volume falls, the same overhead is divided among fewer units, so each unit looks more expensive, and the next customer or product line walks away. Companies break that spiral on purpose: they close a plant or retire a product line deliberately, at a time of their choosing. A public network has nobody doing that job, so it simply thins out.

For now, Britain's change is progressive. The research foundation Nesta estimates that 86 percent of the poorest households will come out ahead, against 24 percent of the wealthiest. It also makes running an electric heat pump look cheaper by comparison. But follow the arithmetic forward. As more homes switch, the same fixed bill is spread over fewer and fewer units of gas, prices climb, and the climb pushes still more people to leave. The households left at the end are not random. They are renters who cannot install a heat pump in someone else's building, retirees with no appetite for a major renovation and families who could not afford the upfront cost.

California has already modeled this. A study for the state's Energy Commission warned that as demand falls, the fixed costs of maintaining the system "will be spread over a smaller number of gas sales," raising "the possibility of a feedback effect" and leaving low-income customers "who are less able to electrify" with "a disproportionate share of gas system costs." In one scenario modeled for the commission, with heavy electrification and no plan, remaining households' gas bills passed $600 a month by 2050; a deliberate transition strategy cut that by more than half.

The same pattern is playing out on copper phone lines. When AT&T asked California regulators to release it from its duty as the carrier of last resort in parts of the state, the commission received thousands of public comments. In 2024 it said no, after people at its public forums described mobile and internet calling as unreliable. Those still relying on a landline are, almost by definition, the people for whom the alternatives have not worked, and they are rarely in a position to argue with a phone company.

Whether households leave because of prices, policy or preference, many will leave. A country that cannot retire aging infrastructure will pay for it forever. The trouble is that markets organize the exit in the worst possible order. The well-off, the well-informed and the homeowners go first, because they can. Whoever remains inherits a growing bill for a shrinking system, and the system frays everywhere at once instead of closing anywhere in particular.

A planned retreat looks different. It recovers the cost of old pipes while there are still many customers to share it, rather than loading it onto the last few, an approach California's study examined under the dry name of accelerated depreciation. It retires networks neighborhood by neighborhood, so that whole streets can switch together and crews stop maintaining mains that serve a handful of houses. It puts renters and low-income households at the front of the line for help to switch, not at the back. And where a lifeline service must be kept for a small number of people, whether a gas main or a copper phone line, it treats that as a public commitment paid for broadly, not a surcharge on whoever could not leave.

Every transition is celebrated through its pioneers: the first rooftop panels, the first heat pumps, the first homes to cut the cord. A fairer test is who is still connected at the end, and what they are being asked to pay. Britain has stumbled onto the question by trying to make one bill fairer. America has time to answer it on purpose.
