John C. Bogle founded the Vanguard Group, and this book is his case for the index fund in its simplest form. It was first published in April 2007. The edition reviewed here is the tenth anniversary edition, published by Wiley in October 2017, which the publisher says has updated data and two new chapters, one on asset allocation and one on retirement investing.

## The short answer

The clearest statement of why costs matter in investing, from the person most associated with the index fund. The argument is arithmetic, not prediction: investors as a group earn the market's return, less what they pay to invest. It is persuasive and short. It is also repetitive, focused on the United States, and written by a man who built a company on the idea.

## Who it is for

- Investors who pay for actively managed funds and have never looked closely at what the fees add up to.
- Beginners who want to understand why index funds are so often recommended, not only that they are.
- Readers who like an argument made with numbers.

## Who it is not for

- Readers who are already convinced and want help with the next step, such as choosing accounts.
- Readers outside the United States who need local detail on funds and tax.
- Anyone who wants a varied read: this is one idea, argued from many angles.

## Key ideas

- **Own the whole market at very low cost.** The publisher's summary of the strategy is to buy and hold a fund that tracks a broad stock market index.
- **Costs come out of the investor's share.** The line most often quoted from the book is "the more the managers and brokers take, the less investors make." Trying to beat the market is, in Bogle's words, "a loser's game".
- **Where returns come from.** The book breaks stock returns into three sources: dividend yield, earnings growth and the change in what the market is willing to pay. The first two reflect business results; the third is opinion and tends to wash out over long periods.
- **Costs compound too.** Bogle sets what he calls the magic of compounding returns against the tyranny of compounding costs.
- **Yesterday's winners.** Chapters on selecting long-term winners and on reversion to the mean argue that picking funds by past performance is unreliable.
- **Other products, examined.** There are chapters on bond funds, on exchange-traded funds, and on index funds that promise to beat the market.
- **Support from others.** One chapter asks what Benjamin Graham would have thought about indexing, and the book quotes investors and academics including Warren Buffett, Paul Samuelson and Burton Malkiel.

## What works

- **The argument does not depend on forecasts.** It follows from how costs work, which is why it has aged well.
- **It is short and focused.** One sitting is enough to get the core of it.
- **It calls on other voices,** so the reader is not asked to take the author's word alone.
- **The newer chapters help.** Asset allocation and retirement were gaps in a book that otherwise deals only with which funds to hold.

## What to be aware of

- **It repeats itself.** The same conclusion arrives chapter after chapter. Some readers find this reinforcing; others will skim.
- **The author is not neutral.** Bogle founded the best-known index fund company. The arithmetic stands on its own, but it is worth knowing who is making the case.
- **It is about the United States.** The data, funds and tax discussion are American.
- **The figures stop in the mid-2010s.** The edition dates from 2017.
- **"Guarantee" in the subtitle needs reading with care.** An index fund delivers the market's return less a small cost. The market's return itself can be poor or negative for long stretches.

## The verdict

If you read one book on why low costs matter, this is a strong choice. Read the first few chapters closely and the rest more quickly. ## FAQ

### What is the difference between the 2007 and 2017 editions?

According to Wiley, the tenth anniversary edition has updated data and new information, plus two new chapters on asset allocation and retirement investing.

### Is the book only about Vanguard funds?

No. The argument is about low-cost, broad index funds in general. Bogle founded Vanguard, and that background is part of the book.

### Is it suitable for a beginner?

Yes. It is written in plain language. Some chapters go through a lot of figures, which can be skimmed without losing the argument.
