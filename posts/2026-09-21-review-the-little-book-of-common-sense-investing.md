John C. Bogle founded the Vanguard Group, and this book is his case for the index fund in its simplest form. It was first published in April 2007. The edition reviewed here is the tenth anniversary edition, published by Wiley in October 2017, which the publisher says brings the figures up to date and adds two chapters, on asset allocation and on investing for retirement.

## The short answer

The clearest statement of why costs matter in investing, from the person most associated with the index fund. The argument is arithmetic, not prediction: investors as a group earn the market's return, less what they pay to invest, so the cheapest way of owning the whole market must beat the average of everything else. It is persuasive and short. It is also repetitive, focused on the United States, written by a man who built a company on the idea, and its figures stop in 2016.

## Who it is for

- Investors who pay for actively managed funds and have never looked closely at what the fees add up to.
- Beginners who want to understand why index funds are so often recommended, not only that they are.
- Readers who like an argument made with numbers.
- Anyone being sold a fund on the strength of its recent performance.

## Who it is not for

- Readers who are already convinced and want help with the next step, such as choosing accounts.
- Readers outside the United States who need local detail on funds and tax.
- Anyone who wants a varied read: this is one idea, argued from many angles.

## How the book is organised

Wiley lists 304 pages. The contents page of the 2017 edition shows an introduction and twenty short chapters, and they fall into a clear order. Chapters one to three set out how the stock market creates value ("A Parable", "Rational Exuberance", "Cast Your Lot with Business"). Chapters four to nine count what investors lose on the way: fund costs, dividends, "The Grand Illusion", taxes, and a chapter on lower future returns ("When the Good Times No Longer Roll"). Chapters ten to thirteen deal with the hope of picking winning funds or advisers. Chapters fourteen to sixteen examine bond funds, exchange-traded funds and "Index Funds That Promise to Beat the Market". The rest covers what Benjamin Graham would have thought of indexing, two chapters on asset allocation, and a closing chapter of advice.

In the first chapter, which the publisher makes available as an excerpt, the text ends with a boxed section titled "Don't Take My Word for It", quoting other investors and academics in support.

## Key ideas

### The Gotrocks parable

The book opens with a story Bogle adapts from Warren Buffett's 2005 letter to Berkshire Hathaway shareholders. A family called the Gotrocks owns every company in the United States and grows richer each year from the earnings and dividends. Then "Helpers" arrive. Brokers persuade some cousins to trade shares with each other, for a commission. When that makes the family poorer, money managers offer to pick the shares, for a fee. When that fails too, consultants offer to pick the managers. Each layer takes a slice, and the trading adds tax.

In the end a wise uncle tells the family to get rid of all the Helpers and go back to holding everything. "That is exactly what an index fund does," Bogle writes. He ends on the moral Buffett drew from the story, which is that the more investors as a group move their money around, the less of the return they keep.

The parable carries the whole argument. Before costs, investors together own the market, so together they earn the market's return. After costs, they must earn less, by exactly the amount of the costs.

### Why the industry cannot say this

Bogle draws a blunt conclusion from the story: the people who make their living from investing and the people whose money is invested want opposite things. The business does well by urging its customers, "Don't just stand there. Do something." The customers, taken together, do well by turning the advice round: "Don't do something. Just stand there."

### Where returns come from

The book breaks stock returns into three sources: the dividend yield, the growth in company earnings, and the change in what the market is willing to pay for those earnings. The first two reflect what businesses do. The third reflects opinion, and the publisher's summary gives Bogle's view of it: over a long enough period, what companies actually earn and pay out counts for more than what the market expected of them. The same three parts are his tool for working out what returns it is reasonable to expect over the coming decade, which is where the chapter on lower future returns comes in.

### Costs compound too

Bogle sets "the magic of compounding returns" against "the tyranny of compounding costs". A small yearly charge looks harmless because it is quoted as a percentage of your money, not of your return, and because its effect builds slowly.

*An illustration with round numbers, not taken from the book:* 10,000 left for 40 years at 7% a year grows to about 150,000. If costs take 2 percentage points a year, so that it grows at 5%, it reaches about 70,000. The 2% charge has consumed more than half of the final sum.

### Past winners are a poor guide

The chapters "Selecting Long-Term Winners" and "Reversion to the Mean" take on the natural response: accept that the average fund loses, and pick an above-average one. Reversion to the mean is the tendency of unusually good results to drift back towards average. The first chapter already makes the point through a former head of Harvard's endowment, whom Bogle quotes as saying that most people who believe they can find winning managers are mistaken, and that the share of managers who fall short of their benchmark is 85 to 90 percent.

### Even index products can be misused

Bogle does not approve of everything with "index" in its name. He gives a chapter to exchange-traded funds, which are index funds that can be bought and sold all day like shares, and another to funds that keep the index label while claiming they can do better than the market. The publisher's summary names his worry: an index fund is meant to be held, and investors who trade in and out of one throw away the advantage it gave them.

## What works

- **The argument does not depend on forecasts.** It follows from how costs work, which is why it has aged well.
- **It is short and focused.** The first chapter alone gives the core of it.
- **It calls on other voices.** The book quotes Warren Buffett, Paul Samuelson, Burton Malkiel, David Swensen and others, so the reader is not asked to take the author's word alone.
- **It counts taxes and frequent trading as costs,** not only fund fees.
- **The newer chapters help.** Asset allocation and retirement were gaps in a book that otherwise deals only with which funds to hold.

## What to be aware of

- **It repeats itself.** The same conclusion arrives chapter after chapter. Some readers find this reinforcing; others will skim from about the halfway point.
- **The author is not neutral.** Bogle founded the best-known index fund company. The arithmetic stands on its own, but it is worth knowing who is making the case.
- **The arithmetic proves less than it seems.** It shows that the average actively managed pound or dollar must lag the market after costs. It does not show that no manager can win, only that choosing one in advance is hard.
- **It is about the United States.** The data, the funds and the tax discussion are American, and the central recommendation is a fund holding American companies.
- **The figures stop in 2016,** and Bogle died in 2019, so there will be no further revision by him.
- **"Guarantee" in the subtitle needs reading with care.** An index fund delivers the market's return less a small cost. The market's return itself can be poor or negative for long stretches.

## How it compares

[The Simple Path to Wealth](../2026-09-07-review-the-simple-path-to-wealth/) gives the same recommendation in a friendlier voice and with more on what to do at each stage of life, but with much less evidence. [A Random Walk Down Wall Street](../2026-09-28-review-a-random-walk-down-wall-street/) is the fuller treatment: Malkiel also explains why share prices are so hard to predict, where Bogle mostly argues from costs. Bogle quotes Malkiel in his own first chapter.

One chapter asks what Benjamin Graham would have thought about indexing. Graham's own book is reviewed here: [The Intelligent Investor](../2026-10-05-review-the-intelligent-investor/). It is the place to go for the opposite skill, judging individual companies.

On edition differences: the 2007 first edition makes the same argument with older figures and without the chapters on asset allocation and retirement. Buy the 2017 edition if you have the choice.

## The verdict

If you read one book on why low costs matter, this is a strong choice. Read the first five chapters closely and the rest more quickly. Readers who already own index funds and understand why will learn less from it than from a book about what to do next.

## FAQ

### What is the difference between the 2007 and 2017 editions?

According to Wiley, the tenth anniversary edition brings the figures up to date and adds two chapters, one on asset allocation and one on investing for retirement.

### Is the book only about Vanguard funds?

No. The argument is about low-cost, broad index funds in general. Bogle founded Vanguard, and that background is part of the book.

### Is it suitable for a beginner?

Yes. It is written in plain language. Some chapters go through a lot of figures, which can be skimmed without losing the argument.

### Can I sample it before buying?

Yes. Wiley publishes the contents page and the first chapter, the Gotrocks parable, as free excerpts.
