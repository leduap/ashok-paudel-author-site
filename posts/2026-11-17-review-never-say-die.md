Never Say Die: A Memoir is Ke Huy Quan's account of his life, from a childhood leaving Vietnam to child stardom, a long absence from acting, and a return in middle age. Grand Central Publishing brought out the US hardcover on 15 September 2026 and lists it at 256 pages. A UK edition is published separately, so a British copy may not match this one page for page. The title is not unique. Other books are called Never Say Die. This review is of Quan's memoir, the recent one with that subtitle.

Kirkus and the publisher describe the life in enough detail to say what the book covers. A table of contents was not found, so there is no chapter-by-chapter reading below. Where a detail comes from an interview rather than from a review of the book, that is said.

## The short answer

A grateful, chronological memoir. Quan tells a refugee childhood, a sudden career in two famous 1980s films, the years when the parts stopped, and the film that brought him back, Everything Everywhere All at Once, for which he won an Academy Award. Kirkus calls the tone witty and thankful, and says he does not skip the hard middle. The limit is the limit of this kind of book. It is his memory, shaped toward perseverance, and it is not a study of how Hollywood casts Asian actors, except as that system happened to him. Read it for the life. Do not read it as proof that patience is a plan.

## Who it is for

- Readers who grew up with Indiana Jones and the Temple of Doom or The Goonies and want the child's version of those sets.
- Readers interested in a refugee childhood told by the person who lived it, including a separation from part of his family.
- Anyone who has watched a public comeback and wants the years nobody was watching.
- Readers who like a warm voice and will accept that warmth as a choice, not as the absence of pain.

## Who it is not for

- Readers who want an exposé of studios, agents or casting. The industry is the weather in this book, not the subject of an inquiry.
- Readers who need dates and contracts checked against other people's records. This is a memoir.
- Anyone looking for a method. "Do not give up" is the title's moral. It is not a set of steps, and it did not work on a timetable he controlled.
- Readers who want the other people in the story at equal length. His parents, his wife and his directors appear as he remembers them.

## How the book is organised

No contents page was opened. Kirkus describes a straight line through the life, and the publisher's description follows the same line, which is as much structure as this review will claim.

The first part is Vietnam in the 1970s, a flight, a refugee camp in Hong Kong, and Los Angeles. The middle is the two films and then what Quan calls, according to Kirkus, "the lost years": film school, work behind the camera, marriage. The last part is the return, in his forties, and the awards. An interview reported by Kirkus uses a different phrase, "the quiet years", for the same stretch, and Quan said those pages were the hardest to write. Either name, the book is built to make the gap as important as the fame on either side of it.

Grand Central lists 256 pages. That is a short book for a life with this many chapters in it. The prose, on the evidence of the reviews, moves quickly and does not stop to build a theory.

## Key ideas

### Leaving, and a year without his mother

Kirkus is more precise than the jacket about the start. Quan is ethnically Chinese. After the war, he and his parents and eight siblings were persecuted in Vietnam. They reached a refugee camp in Hong Kong and later Los Angeles. He describes a frightening passage, and a separation of about a year from his mother and three of his siblings. The publisher's note puts the childhood in Los Angeles, after the family had reached America. Kirkus says the camp and the uncertainty are written with care, not skipped in a rush to the movie set. Publishers Weekly, quoted on the publisher's page, singles out the monotony of the camp as something the book actually sits in. The Hollywood story starts only after that. The book, if the reviews are right, refuses to treat the films as the beginning of the person.

### 1982, and two films that fixed a face

Kirkus says Quan dates a break in his life to 1982, when a casual audition won him the part of Short Round. The film, out in 1984, was Indiana Jones and the Temple of Doom. The Goonies followed in 1985. He played Data. He was a child. The publisher's note says he was twelve at the Indiana Jones casting, and that he had no acting experience. Kirkus says the affectionate set-pieces are Spielberg as a mentor, Harrison Ford teaching him to swim, and time with the other children in a motel. They are also the last easy success for a long time.

### The years with no parts

After The Goonies the roles dried up. Kirkus says he does not wave this away. He went to film school at the University of Southern California, worked as a stunt performer and an assistant director, and met Echo, who became his wife. The publisher's note adds that he graduated from USC's School of Cinematic Arts in 1999, and that the behind-the-camera work included action choreography. The jacket copy mentions work for the director Wong Kar Wai. In the Kirkus interview he called this the part of the book he most dreaded writing: rejection, not knowing who he was without the work, and staying with it when nobody was looking. The title, he told that interviewer, was suggested to him and seemed right because it nodded at The Goonies and at stubbornness. Only while writing, he said, did he see that the example had been his father, who had not given up. The interview is not the book. It is the author's account of what the middle of the book cost him.

### Three versions of one man

The return is Everything Everywhere All at Once (2022). Kirkus says Quan was required to play three versions of Waymond Wang, each with a different way of moving, and that the book tries to show both the difficulty and the pleasure of that. The publisher's note records what followed: the Academy Award, the Golden Globe and the Screen Actors Guild award for best supporting actor, then later work, including a season of the series Loki, the film Love Hurts, and a voice in Zootopia 2. The jacket says he was forty-nine when he returned to acting. The "about the author" note and the jacket are not the same sentence, and this review will not force them to be. The public fact the memoir is built toward is the return, and the awards that made the return news.

### Gratitude is the style, and it is doing work

Kirkus's summary words are wit, charm and gratitude. The book is not, on that review, a settling of scores. Quan likes the people who helped him, and he includes the years that hurt. A memoir written in that key will move readers who want a comeback to mean something about character. It will frustrate readers who want the industry's indifference examined as a system, rather than as a personal winter that ended. Both responses fit the book the reviews describe. The subtitle is "a memoir". It is not "a report".

## What works

- **The hard years are in the book.** Kirkus specifically praises him for not omitting them, and he has said they were the pages he found most difficult.
- **The childhood is not a prologue.** The camp, the separation and the uncertainty are treated as a story of their own.
- **The famous anecdotes are his.** Ford, Spielberg and the motel are told from the child who was there, which is the only reason to prefer this book to a magazine profile.
- **The tone is consistent.** Reviewers quoted by the publisher, including Kirkus, Publishers Weekly and Booklist, describe a likeable, thoughtful voice. None of the notices opened here calls it bitter or vague.
- **It is short.** At 256 pages it can be read as one life, not as a career archive.

## What to be aware of

- **It is his memory, arranged as encouragement.** The title, by his own account in the interview, is about not giving up. A life that ends in an Oscar will make perseverance look more reliable than it is. Most people who wait twenty years are not handed a film like that at the end.
- **Hollywood's narrowness is felt, and not really investigated.** Kirkus says the parts stopped. The publisher says the work dried up and he went behind the camera. A reader can see a pattern in the casting of Asian actors. The book, as reviewed, does not step back to measure that pattern. It stays with one man.
- **No contents page was available.** The order above is the order Kirkus and the publisher recount. It may not be the order of the chapters, and chapter titles are not guessed.
- **Other people are supporting players.** Echo, his parents and his siblings are in the story as he tells it. They have not written this book. The year apart from his mother is his version of a family event.
- **There is more than one edition.** This review is of the Grand Central hardcover of 15 September 2026, 256 pages. A UK publisher lists its own edition. Match the edition if you are checking a quotation.
- **The warmth can sand down a scene.** Kirkus's word is gratitude. Gratitude is a virtue in a person and a risk in a memoir, because it decides in advance that the helpers were help. The reviews opened here do not cite a passage where he turns that warmth off for long.

## How it compares

[Born a Crime](../2026-09-02-review-born-a-crime/) is the closest book on this site: a performer writing about a childhood under a political order that was not built for him, and about the later public life. Trevor Noah's book is funnier, harsher, and more interested in how poverty holds people in place. Quan's, on the reviews, is gentler and more grateful, and its second half is about an industry rather than about a country. Both are one man's account. Neither is a history of the system it passed through.

[Strangers](../2026-09-16-review-strangers/) is a reminder from a very different life that a family memoir is one side. Echo appears here as the person he met and married. She did not write this book.

## The verdict

A clear, affectionate memoir of a life with a long hole in the middle of the fame. It is worth reading for the camp, the sets and the years of no work, told by the person who was there. It is not a manual for getting an Oscar, and it is not a history of who else was left waiting.

## FAQ

### Which book is this?

Ke Huy Quan's memoir, subtitled A Memoir, published by Grand Central on 15 September 2026, 256 pages. Other books share the title Never Say Die. This is the 2026 one by the actor.

### What are "the lost years"?

Kirkus's name, taken from Quan, for the long period after The Goonies when acting work disappeared. He studied at the University of Southern California, graduating in 1999 according to the publisher, and worked behind the camera, including as a stunt performer, assistant director and action choreographer. In an interview he called the same period the quiet years, and said they were the hardest part of the book to write.

### Where does the title come from?

He told an interviewer, in a conversation reported by Kirkus on 14 September 2026, that the title was suggested to him, that it winked at The Goonies, and that while writing he came to see it as his father's example of not giving up. That is his account of the title, not a line checked against the title page.

### Did he narrate the story as well as write it?

The print book is the one reviewed here. A separate audiobook exists. This review is about the memoir as Kirkus and the publisher describe the text, not about a performance of it.
