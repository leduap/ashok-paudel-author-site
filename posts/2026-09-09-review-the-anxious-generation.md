The Anxious Generation is a book about children and phones, not about money. It belongs on a finance site for one reason: it is a case study in how to weigh evidence and how to act when a problem can only be solved by many people moving together. Penguin Press published it on 26 March 2024. More than two years on, it was number 10 on the New York Times hardcover nonfiction list dated 6 September 2026, in its 120th week.

## The short answer

Jonathan Haidt, a social psychologist, argues that two changes combined to damage the mental health of young people: childhood moved indoors and under supervision, and then it moved onto smartphones. He sets out the figures, explains the mechanisms he believes are at work, and ends with four proposals. The book is organised, readable and has clearly influenced schools and lawmakers. Its central claim of cause is contested by other researchers, who say the data show an association and not proof. Read it as a strong argument for one side of a live debate, and read the critics too.

## Who it is for

- Parents deciding when a child should have a smartphone or social media accounts.
- Teachers, school leaders and governors considering rules on phones.
- Readers who want to follow a public argument that is shaping law in several countries.
- Anyone interested in how groups escape situations where no individual can afford to act alone.

## Who it is not for

- Readers who want a neutral survey of the research. This is advocacy by a scholar with a position.
- People looking for clinical guidance on a child who is unwell. It is not a treatment manual.
- Readers outside wealthy, English-speaking countries, whose school systems and laws may differ from the ones discussed.
- Anyone expecting a finance or career book.

## How the book is organised

The publisher lists 400 pages. The author's website gives the contents as thirteen chapters in four parts.

Part 1, "A Tidal Wave", is a single chapter, "The Surge of Suffering", which presents the mental health figures. Part 2, "The Backstory: The Decline of Play-Based Childhood", has three chapters on what children need, on risky play, and on puberty and the passage to adulthood. Part 3, "The Great Rewiring: The Rise of Phone-Based Childhood", has four: "The Four Basic Harms", one chapter on girls, one on boys, and "Spiritual Degradation and Elevation". Part 4, "Collective Action for Healthier Childhood", runs through what governments and technology companies, schools, and parents can each do, and closes with a chapter called "Bring Childhood Back to Earth".

The order is that of a brief: evidence of a problem, a theory of its causes, and remedies. The same website hosts extra material that was cut from each chapter for length, along with a list of corrections. This review is written from those pages, the publisher's details and the published responses listed under Sources.

## Key ideas

### Something changed in the early 2010s

The book starts from a pattern in the figures. According to the publisher, measures of adolescent depression, anxiety, self-harm and suicide had been steady or improving for over a decade and then rose steeply in the early 2010s. The Wikipedia summary of the book adds that Haidt finds the rise in several developed countries at once, finds much less of it among people over thirty, and rejects the 2008 financial crisis as the explanation because earlier downturns did not produce the same effect. Timing and breadth are the core of his case.

### Children need risk in order to grow

The second part argues that the ground was prepared before phones arrived. Haidt's claim is that children get stronger through manageable difficulty: unsupervised play, minor injuries, quarrels settled without an adult. From the 1980s, he says, adults reduced that exposure in the name of safety, while shorter school breaks and car-centred streets cut the opportunities further. A child with fewer chances to roam was then handed a device that made staying in more attractive still.

### Four harms

Chapter 5 names four ways a phone-centred childhood does damage: less time with others in person, less sleep, attention broken into fragments, and addiction. The publisher mentions more than a dozen mechanisms in total, including comparison with others and the spread of distress through social groups. The book treats girls and boys separately. In outline, girls are said to be hurt more by social media, while boys are said to have withdrawn from the physical world into the virtual one.

### A trap that needs a joint exit

The idea with the widest use outside parenting is the "collective action problem". This is a situation where each person's sensible choice produces a result nobody wants. A parent who withholds a phone when every classmate has one may isolate the child, so most parents give in, even those who would prefer otherwise. No family can fix this alone. It takes a shared rule.

*An illustration with round numbers, not taken from the book:* in a class of 30, suppose 25 sets of parents would prefer their children not to have smartphones yet. If each decides separately, each sees that the others are likely to buy one, and buys one too. All 30 children end up with phones although 25 families wanted the opposite. If the 25 agree a date together, their children are in the majority and the pressure runs the other way.

Readers will recognise the same structure in bank runs and in any market where everyone sells because everyone else is selling.

### Four new norms

Haidt's remedies, as listed on his website, are: no smartphones before high school, with a basic phone for calls in the meantime; no social media until 16; schools free of phones for the whole school day; and a larger share of independence, unsupervised play and real responsibility away from screens. The first three restrict. The fourth gives something back, and the author presents it as essential and not optional.

## What works

- **The structure is easy to follow.** Problem, causes and remedies are kept in separate parts, so a reader can accept one and question another.
- **It names a mechanism, not only a trend.** The collective action argument explains why well-meaning parents feel stuck, which simple alarm about screens does not.
- **The proposals are specific.** Ages and settings are stated, so they can be adopted, tested or rejected.
- **It is open to checking.** The author publishes his notes, figures, evidence files and a list of errors online.
- **It pairs limits with freedom.** The call for more unsupervised play keeps the book from being only a list of prohibitions.

## What to be aware of

- **Cause is disputed.** The responses below are taken from the summary of the book's reception in its Wikipedia entry. In a review for the journal Nature, the psychologist Candice Odgers wrote that the claim that digital technology is rewiring children's brains and producing an epidemic is not supported by the science, and that the link could run the other way, with unhappy teenagers using social media more. Haidt has replied that he and a colleague have gathered experimental studies that support him.
- **Some of the figures can be read differently.** A New York Times writer pointed out that changes in screening guidelines and in how hospitals record self-harm could account for part of the rise, and that trends differ between countries.
- **The parenting half rests on less evidence.** The Guardian's reviewer, who was otherwise persuaded, found the overprotection argument far less well supported than the material on phones.
- **Critics see a moral panic.** The sociologist Mike Males and others argue that concentrating on phones draws attention from better-established causes of adolescent distress, such as trouble at home.
- **It is a campaign as well as a book.** The author's website now belongs to an organisation that promotes the four norms, seeks donations and sells a companion book for younger readers. That is not a fault, but it means the author is an advocate.
- **Things have moved since 2024.** Laws and school policies have changed in many places since publication, so the policy chapters describe a starting point.

## How it compares

Haidt's earlier book with Greg Lukianoff, The Coddling of the American Mind, supplies the overprotection argument that Part 2 builds on. The idea that people gain strength from stress comes from the book Antifragile, which the Wikipedia summary identifies as an inspiration.

No other review on this site covers the same subject. The nearest in method is [The Psychology of Money](../2026-09-14-review-the-psychology-of-money/), which also argues that behaviour is shaped by environment and peers more than by information. A useful contrast is [A Random Walk Down Wall Street](../2026-09-28-review-a-random-walk-down-wall-street/), which spends much of its length testing claims against data and is cautious about stories that explain a trend neatly. That habit is worth bringing to this book.

## The verdict

A forceful, well-arranged argument that has changed what many schools and parents do. Its proposals are modest enough that a reader may find them reasonable even without being convinced on cause. On the science, it is one side of an unsettled question, and it should be read with that in mind.

## FAQ

### Does the book prove that smartphones cause mental illness in teenagers?

It argues that they do. Several researchers who study the subject say the evidence shows a link and falls short of proof. The author disagrees and points to experimental studies.

### What are the four norms?

No smartphones before high school, no social media until 16, phone-free schools, and more independence and unsupervised play away from screens.

### Is it only for parents?

No. The parts on collective action and on policy are relevant to teachers, school leaders and anyone following the debate about regulating technology.

### How long is it?

The publisher lists 400 pages, in thirteen chapters.
