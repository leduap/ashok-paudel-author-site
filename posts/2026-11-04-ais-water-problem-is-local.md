Google's data centers in Council Bluffs, Iowa, consumed 1.35 billion gallons of water in 2025, according to the company's own reporting. The Environmental Protection Agency's rule of thumb is that an average American family uses about 300 gallons a day at home. By that measure, one cluster of server halls used about a year's household water for 12,000 families. Google's campus in The Dalles, Oregon, consumed 469 million gallons.

Nationally, the figures really are modest. A report for the Energy Department by Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory estimated that American data centers directly consumed about 17 billion gallons of water in 2023. That was triple their 2014 use, but still small. By Google's own comparison, U.S. data centers use less than 1 percent of the water Americans put on their lawns. The lab projects that the largest data centers alone could consume about 16 billion to 33 billion gallons a year by 2028.

Both things are true at once: the national total is small, and one town's share is not. That is why the debate over AI's water use keeps going in circles. Water isn't a global commodity like oil. It comes from a particular aquifer or river and is treated by a particular city's utility. It is also scarcest in the same summer weeks when servers run hottest. That local scale is where the real decisions get made: a city council voting on a permit, a utility planning a new well. It is also where the debate has the least information, because many of the largest operators report only regional or company-wide totals.

As a controller, I learned early that averages are where problems hide. A company can report healthy margins overall while one plant loses money every month, and you only find it by breaking the numbers down by site and by month. Water works the same way. A corporate total or a per-query average tells a town almost nothing about what will happen to its own supply in August.

Two changes would turn a shouting match into a negotiation.

The first is disclosure. Every large data center should publish how much water it withdraws and consumes, broken down by site and by season, and say where that water comes from: the drinking-water system, groundwater or recycled wastewater. It can be done: Google's report lists withdrawal and consumption for each of its campuses, though the figures are self-reported. What one company does voluntarily should become the standard for every facility above a modest size, with independent checks.

Europe is a cautionary tale. Under its Energy Efficiency Directive, every data center with at least 500 kilowatts of IT power has to report its annual water use to a European database. But the individual records are confidential, and the public sees only national and European totals. Europe collected exactly the right data, then kept it from the people who need it most. American states and cities can do better.

The second change is price. Most utilities price water to recover the cost of pumping and treating it, not to reflect how scarce it is in a dry August. Utilities and states could charge large users more for water drawn in peak months or from stressed sources, and less for recycled water or water stored in the wet season. Data-center operators are among the world's best-capitalized companies. Given a clear price signal, they will pick their cooling designs, sites and water sources to match. Some already do. Google says it paid for an aquifer storage system in The Dalles that banks winter water for summer use, then gave it to the city at no cost. A price would make deals like that the norm instead of a matter of corporate goodwill.

None of this is anti-growth. Quite the opposite. A community that can't see the numbers has every reason to say no. A town that knows how much water a facility will use, in which months, and what the operator will pay for it can weigh that against the jobs and the tax base and strike a deal. Secrecy and underpricing produce protests and delays. Transparency and a fair price produce permits.

The national and per-query numbers will keep looking reassuring. The question worth answering is smaller and harder: how much water, from where, in which month, in this town? Every operator already knows the answer. They should tell us.
