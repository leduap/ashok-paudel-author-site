A Hymn to Life: Shame Has to Change Sides is Gisèle Pelicot's memoir of her marriage and of the criminal case that made her name public in 2024. Judith Perrignon, a journalist, wrote it with her. Natasha Lehrer and Ruth Diver carried the book into English. Penguin Press published the US hardcover on 17 February 2026 and lists it at 256 pages. The Guardian reviewed the Bodley Head edition, also February 2026, at £22, ISBN 9781847928962 on the British listing. The New York Review of Books lists the Penguin Press book at 246 pages. Those two counts are both in sources opened for this review. They have not been checked against a printed copy.

The publisher calls it an instant New York Times bestseller. This review does not retell the case in graphic detail. The book does go into that detail. A reader should know that before opening it.

## The short answer

A memoir in two times at once. One track is the investigation and the trial in Avignon. The other is a life of about seventy years: a childhood, a long marriage, work, children, and the ordinary days she has had to re-read in the light of what the police found. Pelicot's public sentence was that shame had to change sides, from the person attacked to the people who attacked her. The book is that argument, told from inside a marriage she had believed to be a happy one. It is also a mediated book, written with a professional writer and then translated, by a woman who cannot describe the assaults from memory because she was drugged. Read it if you want her account of how a private life became a public case, and how she refused to let other people finish the story for her. Do not read it for a guide to French criminal law, or for a single lesson that fits every survivor.

No chapter list was opened. The shape below comes from the New York Review of Books, the Guardian, Kirkus and the publisher. Where they differ, the difference is noted.

## Who it is for

- Readers who followed the Avignon trial and want her version, not only the reporting.
- Readers interested in how a person rebuilds a past once a central fact of it has been shown to be false.
- Readers who can face explicit material about sexual violence, including violence she did not know was happening.
- Readers who want a memoir that argues with the people urging her toward a tidier reaction, including people who love her.

## Who it is not for

- Anyone looking for a detached history of the case, with every defendant's record set out.
- Readers who need the narrator to have seen the events she describes. She is writing about assaults she did not experience as a conscious witness.
- Readers who want a manual for changing the law. The book has a public moral. It is not a statute.
- Anyone who would rather not read the medical and sexual detail. The reviews are clear that she does not leave it out.

## How the book is organised

Elaine Blair, in the New York Review of Books, describes the method as two lines that keep meeting. The investigation moves forward from the autumn of 2020. Memory moves back through childhood, youth and the marriage, and then the new facts are laid against the old memories. The Guardian says the 2024 trial itself occupies only the last fifth of the book. Most of the pages, on that account, are the life before the courtroom and the months of trying to understand the marriage.

Blair says Pelicot at first wanted the trial closed, which is the usual practice in French rape cases and which she thought would protect her. She later decided a closed court would leave her alone with the accused. The trial opened in Avignon in September 2024. The publisher says it lasted three and a half months. Blair says almost four. It was public because she asked for that.

There is no contents page in the sources opened here, so this review does not name chapters or pretend to know what each one argues.

## Key ideas

### The day the marriage ended, which was not the day it looked finished

In October 2020, Blair writes, Pelicot thought she was going to a police station because her husband, Dominique, had been caught filming under women's skirts in a supermarket. He had already told her about that, and she had set conditions: apologies, a therapist, and an end to the marriage if it happened again. An officer then told her the arrest was for a much more serious crime. She was shown photographs of unconscious assaults in her own bedroom. Blair says the police had thousands of images, that the drugging and the assaults had gone on for years, and that strangers had been invited in. The publisher's wording is the same in outline: nearly a decade, in the home, without her knowledge. Kirkus quotes the officer's warning that she was about to be shown pictures and films she would not like. The memoir begins, in other words, at the moment a smaller disgrace turns out to be the edge of a much larger one.

### She audits her own life

Blair's point is that the book is less a trial narrative than a re-examination of fifty years. Pelicot and Dominique met young. She is sure, Blair says, that the early happiness was real, and the book has to live with that certainty next to the later crimes. The Guardian calls this a detective story told on herself: which memory was a clue, and which was simply a life. Her mother died when she was nine. She left school young, worked as a secretary, and rose, in Blair's account, into management, while his work and his debts were unsteady. They moved to Mazan, in Provence, in 2013. The ordinary career is the material she uses to ask how she missed what was done in her own house.

### Shame, and who is supposed to carry it

At the opening of the trial she said that shame had to change sides. The publisher treats that sentence as the book's public purpose: victims of sexual violence have no reason to carry the disgrace that belongs to the perpetrators. Blair quotes her on a second shame, the shame of having understood nothing. The Guardian quotes a longer misery of the same kind, the feeling of looking like a fool, to other people and to herself. The book does not pretend the transfer of shame is easy, or that saying it in court finishes it. Kirkus says she is also hard on lawyers who try to shrink a woman in the name of male domination, and on a press she experienced as greedy at a moment when she wanted time and quiet. The argument is not only aimed at the men in the dock.

### The trial is the last fifth, and it is not the whole moral

The Guardian says that by the time the court sits, the book has already done most of its work, and that Pelicot will not be pushed into a reaction that is not hers. She kept the married name, the review says, partly so grandchildren who share it could have a way to be proud of it. She fought with her daughter Caroline, who has written her own memoir, and for a time two of her three children were not speaking to her, because she still asked them to remember a father she was not ready to reduce to the crimes. The review says they later reconciled. In court, the Guardian says, she looked at photographs of her grandchildren while the films were played, and she credits the women outside the building with getting her through the days. Blair and Kirkus both note that the number of men was larger than the number who stood trial, and that some were never identified. Kirkus reports the sentences as the book gives them: twenty years for Dominique, and between three and fifteen years for the other men who were convicted. Kirkus also says a reader may find the husband's term light. That is the reviewer's judgement, not a legal analysis offered here.

### She will not end where the crowd wants

The Guardian says the early pages can anger a reader. Pelicot did her husband's laundry after the arrest, and worried he would be cold in jail. She writes, the review says, as a woman for whom a husband had been the axis of life, and she resents lectures, from her children and from the court, on the correct way to respond. The book ends, on that account, with a new relationship and a refusal of any general disgust for men. She will not be recruited for a sharper conclusion than the one she has reached.

## What works

- **The structure matches the problem.** A life and an investigation are told so that each memory has to survive the new facts. Blair calls that the book's real form.
- **She will say that she was happy.** The early marriage is not rewritten as a costume for the crimes. That makes the later knowledge harder, and more believable.
- **The public sentence is earned in private pages.** "Shame has to change sides" is not a slogan dropped on the first page and left there. The book shows how difficult the transfer is.
- **She argues with her supporters.** The Guardian's account of the rows with her children, and of her dislike of the word "dignified", keeps the book from being a statue.
- **The translators are part of the achievement.** Blair praises Lehrer and Diver for a plain English that can carry both control and distress. Kirkus names them on the review.

## What to be aware of

- **It is written with someone else.** Judith Perrignon is a journalist, and both Kirkus and the New York Review of Books credit her as the person the memoir was written with. The "I" of the book is Pelicot's, and it has been shaped with a professional writer, then moved from French into English. That is normal for a book of this kind. It is still a layer between the reader and the raw recollection.
- **She cannot remember the assaults.** The Guardian calls this the apparently impossible task of the book: to write about horrors she has no recollection of, because she was unconscious. The knowledge comes from investigators, images and the accused. It is testimony about discovery, not an eyewitness account of the acts.
- **It is one side, and the family does not speak in one voice.** Caroline Pelicot's own memoir is mentioned by the Guardian as part of the rift. This book cannot be the whole household's story.
- **The material is explicit, and it goes beyond the headline.** Blair's essay describes further findings in the investigation, including crimes and allegations involving other people, and symptoms doctors had not connected to the drugging. This review is not going to repeat that detail. Anyone who reads the book should expect it.
- **The page counts do not match.** The publisher and Kirkus say 256 pages. The New York Review of Books says 246. The US and UK hardbacks are different imprints of the same season. This review has not seen either copy.
- **A memoir is not a judgment.** Kirkus reports the sentences and finds the husband's term open to question. The book is Pelicot's account of living through the case. It is not a substitute for the judgment, the statute, or a full record of every defendant.

## How it compares

[Strangers](../2026-09-16-review-strangers/) is the other marriage memoir on this site. It too is one person's story of a life that looked settled and then was not, and the review of it treats the financial claims as one side's account. Pelicot's book is not about money. The shared limit is the same: a memoir can be honest and still be a single view. The events here were tested in a criminal court, which is a check Strangers does not have, and that check does not turn the memoir into a transcript.

[The Body Keeps the Score](../2026-09-30-review-the-body-keeps-the-score/) is this site's long book on trauma, and the review of it says many of its scientific claims have been challenged. Pelicot is not offering a theory of the body or a treatment. She is offering a life. Readers who want a clinical framework will not find it here, and they should not import one from that other book in order to explain her.

## The verdict

A serious book about a public crime, written as a private audit of a marriage. It is clearest when Pelicot is disagreeing with the role assigned to her, and it is hardest where the evidence outruns anything she can remember. Read it for her account and her limits. Do not read it to be told what every such case means.

## FAQ

### Who wrote the English book?

Gisèle Pelicot, with Judith Perrignon. Natasha Lehrer and Ruth Diver translated it from the French. Kirkus and the New York Review of Books both give those credits. The US hardcover is from Penguin Press, 17 February 2026. The Guardian reviewed a Bodley Head edition from the same month.

### What does "shame has to change sides" mean?

It was her statement at the start of the Avignon trial. The publisher's reading is that disgrace should sit with the perpetrators, not with the person who was attacked. The memoir is about how hard that is to live, not only how it sounds in a courtroom.

### How long was the trial, and what were the sentences?

The trial began in Avignon in September 2024. The publisher says it ran for three and a half months. Blair says almost four. Kirkus says the book reports a twenty-year sentence for Dominique Pelicot and sentences of three to fifteen years for the other convicted men, and that some men were never identified.

### Is this review shorter on the case's worst details?

Yes. The structure, the marriage and the trial are taken from reviews and from the publisher. The most explicit findings are not repeated here. The book itself does not spare them, and the reviews say so.
