Everything Is Tuberculosis is John Green's book about a disease that can be cured and that still kills more than a million people a year. The edition this review describes is the Ebury Press hardback of 20 March 2025, listed at 208 pages. The US edition, from Crash Course Books, is dated 18 March 2025. The US publisher's page also says 208 pages. A German listing of the US edition's ISBN gives 198. Kirkus, reviewing the Crash Course book, says 208. The difference is real on the pages that were opened, and it has not been settled by holding a copy. The publisher and Kirkus both treat it as a number-one New York Times bestseller.

Green is best known for novels, including The Fault in Our Stars. With his brother, Hank, he co-created the online series Crash Course. The US edition carries that name as its imprint. He is not a neutral bystander to the subject. The publisher calls him an advocate for wider treatment, and the book is written as one.

## The short answer

A short book that puts one patient's story through a long history. In 2019 Green met Henry Reider, a teenager with tuberculosis, at Lakka Government Hospital in Sierra Leone. Kirkus says the book deals out what happened to Henry chapter by chapter, and fills the gaps with the history of the disease: its old romantic reputation, the useless treatments, the drugs that finally worked, and the reasons those drugs still do not reach everyone. The conclusion Kirkus reports is blunt. Tuberculosis continues because of racism, greed and the economics of public health. Green has put the same point in an interview as "TB is caused by us", meaning by choices about who is worth the cost of a cure. Read it if you want that argument in plain language, tied to a person. Do not read it as a medical textbook, or as a neutral survey. A full list of chapter titles was not found, so this review does not invent one.

## Who it is for

- Readers who know tuberculosis as a disease from old novels and are surprised it still kills on this scale.
- Readers who follow Green and will accept a nonfiction book that is also a campaign.
- Anyone who wants the history of a disease told through patients, including one named patient, rather than through a timeline alone.
- Readers on this site who are used to arguments about cost, and who want to see a cost argument applied to a medicine rather than to a portfolio.

## Who it is not for

- Clinicians looking for treatment protocols, dosages or a review of the trials.
- Readers who want the author off the page. Green is in the story, and his advocacy is the point.
- Anyone hoping for a comforting history in which the cure arrived and the problem closed.
- Readers who need every historical anecdote sourced in the main text. Kirkus describes a readable book. It does not describe a footnoted monograph.

## How the book is organised

No reliable table of contents was opened, so the chapters are not named here. What the reviews agree on is the pattern.

Kirkus says Henry's story is broken up and told across the book, while the surrounding chapters carry the history. The disease was once called consumption and was linked with artists and writers. For a long time the treatments did not work. Sanatoriums confined people, sometimes for years. When streptomycin arrived in the 1940s, Kirkus says, a young American woman who had been shut in such a place since the age of three received some of the early doses and came back out at sixteen. Alongside stories of that kind, Green writes about what treatment costs and about how drug companies behave. There is a postscript. In it, Kirkus reports, he says that if someone had told him, when The Fault in Our Stars appeared, that a decade later he would be thinking about tuberculosis almost all the time, he would have asked whether it was still a disease people got.

The US publisher's description matches the frame: Henry at Lakka, then the scientific and social history, then the claim that today's choices will decide what the disease does next. Penguin's UK page says the same. Two hundred pages, or a little under, is not much room for both a life and a world history. The book is an argument with examples, not an encyclopedia.

## Key ideas

### Henry is the reason the book exists

The publisher, Kirkus and Green himself, in an NPR interview, all start from the same meeting. In 2019 Green and his wife were in Sierra Leone. On the NPR account they had gone to learn about care for mothers, and a visit to the tuberculosis hospital was added at the end. Henry shared a name with Green's son and, Green says, looked about nine. He was seventeen. Malnutrition and the disease had made him small. He showed Green around the hospital, and only afterwards did Green learn that Henry was a patient the staff were worried about, with a strain that was not answering the drugs then available. Kirkus says that for much of the book the treatment is failing, and that Green has been clear in interviews that Henry survived and has been a student at the University of Sierra Leone. Green told NPR that Henry asked him to tell the story. The book's version of that request may be worded differently. The public fact is that a named person is the spine, and that he lived.

### A disease that changed its social meaning

The publisher's line is that tuberculosis was once romanticised as an illness of poets and is now seen as a disease of poverty. Kirkus makes the same turn. The old picture, the sanatorium and the famous deaths, is there so the reader feels the change. A disease that used to be told as a story about sensitivity became a story about who can get a bed and a drug. The history is social before it is clinical.

### The cure arrived, and then the bill did

Kirkus is specific about the twentieth-century change. Ineffective regimes gave way to streptomycin, and a child who had grown up inside an institution could leave. The book then, on Kirkus's account, turns to money: the price of treatment, and the behaviour of pharmaceutical companies. Green's NPR interview states the moral he says is in the book. We know how to kill the bacterium. What remains is a set of human systems that decide some lives are not worth the price. He put a figure on eradication, about $25 billion a year, and said that curing his brother's cancer had cost about 150 times as much as curing Henry's tuberculosis, without anyone calling the cancer treatment a waste. Those numbers are from the interview. He said he was describing the book's argument. The sentences on the page may not match the sentences on the radio.

*An illustration with round numbers, not taken from the book:* a course of drugs at 400, for 1,000 patients in a year, is a drug bill of 400,000 before staff and beds. Calling 400 "not cost-effective" is a decision about the budget. The 400 is made up.

### He uses the audience he already has

Kirkus quotes Green's own name for the advantage his novels gave him: a "curious megaphone". The postscript, as Kirkus describes it, is almost an apology for having taken this long to notice a disease that had not gone away. The US imprint, Crash Course Books, shares its name with the educational channel he and his brother made. The publisher's author note records that channel. A reader is entitled to see the book as part of a larger public project, not as a one-off from a novelist who stumbled into a hospital and left again. That is a strength if you want the argument carried to people who would not read a medical journal. It is a bias if you want the author to be replaceable by any careful reporter.

### Injustice is the cause he wants named

Kirkus summarises the ending in a phrase it attributes to the book: tuberculosis is both a form and an expression of injustice. The publisher says it kills over a million people a year even though it is curable and preventable. "Everything" in the title is the claim that prices, borders and hospital queues belong in the same story as the bacterium. This review has not checked each side story against a chapter. A reader who wants the history without the accusation will be reading against the grain.

## What works

- **One person keeps the history from floating.** Henry's name, age and hospital are in every reliable description of the book.
- **The old romance is used, not repeated.** Consumption as a poet's disease is there so the reader can see what changed when the deaths moved.
- **The drug history has a human scale.** The sanatorium child and the streptomycin doses, as Kirkus tells them, make a medical date into a life sentence that ended.
- **It is short enough to finish.** Around two hundred pages is a choice. Green is not pretending to be exhaustive.
- **He says which side he is on.** An advocate who pretends to be a referee is harder to read than this.

## What to be aware of

- **He is campaigning.** The publisher calls him an advocate, the imprint is tied by name to a channel he co-created, and the NPR interview is a continuation of the same argument. The book is not a disinterested review of the evidence. Treat the moral as his, and check the figures that matter to you against a health agency, not against a novelist.
- **The interview and the book are not the same text.** The $25 billion and the 150-fold comparison were said on NPR. They may be in the book in other words, or with other caveats. This review has not seen the page.
- **There is no chapter list in the sources opened here.** Kirkus describes the pattern, not the contents page. Anything more detailed than that pattern would be a guess, and it has been left out.
- **Henry's story is his, and it is also Green's.** Green says Henry wanted the story told. The telling is still by a famous visitor, not by Henry. A single patient, however carefully drawn, is not a health system.
- **The page count depends on who is counting.** Ebury says 208. The US publisher says 208. Another listing of the US ISBN says 198. Do not treat a page number in a review as a way to find a passage.
- **It will date in the way campaign books date.** Drug prices, aid budgets and which countries are treated as worth the cost move. A book published in March 2025 is already describing a political moment, not a permanent one.

## How it compares

[When Breath Becomes Air](../2026-09-22-review-when-breath-becomes-air/) is the other book on this site about a named person and a disease that reorders a life. Paul Kalanithi's memoir is about becoming a patient, and about how little time a plan can count on. Green is writing about a patient who is not himself, and about a disease we already know how to treat. The feeling of urgency is similar. The ethics are different. Kalanithi cannot choose not to die of his cancer. Green's argument is that Henry's illness was surrounded by choices other people made.

[The Body Keeps the Score](../2026-09-30-review-the-body-keeps-the-score/) is a much longer book about suffering and the body, and the review on this site says researchers have challenged many of its scientific claims. Green is easier to place: he is not building a new theory of mind. He is saying a known cure is being withheld. The risk is not bad science so much as a campaigner's selection of the history.

Nothing else here is about infectious disease. Readers who come from the investing books will recognise the move in the cost chapter, if Kirkus has that chapter right: a price is a decision about whose outcome counts. That is as far as the comparison should go. This book is not advice about money.

## The verdict

A short, pointed book by a writer who wants a curable disease to be treated as a choice. It is a good way to learn why tuberculosis is still here, and a poor place to stop if you need the clinical detail or a critic of Green's own cause. Read it, and then read someone who does not share his microphone.

## FAQ

### Who is Henry?

Henry Reider, a young patient Green met in 2019 at Lakka Government Hospital in Sierra Leone. Green has said Henry was seventeen, not the child he appeared to be, that he survived drug-resistant tuberculosis, and that he asked for his story to be told. Kirkus reports that he went on to study at the University of Sierra Leone.

### Which edition is this?

The Ebury Press hardback, 20 March 2025, listed at 208 pages, ISBN 9781529961423. The US edition is from Crash Course Books, 18 March 2025. Use the Ebury details if you are matching this page. The US page count is given as 208 by the publisher and as 198 on one other listing.

### Is the $25 billion figure from the book?

John Green said it in an NPR interview, as part of what he called the book's argument: about $25 billion a year to eradicate tuberculosis worldwide, and a comparison with the cost of his brother's cancer care. The book's own wording was not checked for this review.

### Why is there no chapter-by-chapter account?

A trustworthy contents list was not found in the publisher pages or the reviews that were opened. Kirkus describes how the book is built, Henry's story alternating with the history, plus a postscript. Naming chapters beyond that would be invention.
