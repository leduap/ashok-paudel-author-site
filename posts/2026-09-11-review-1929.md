Andrew Ross Sorkin's 1929 retells the most famous stock market collapse in American history through the bankers, speculators and politicians who were in the room. Viking published the hardcover on 14 October 2025. It was still on the New York Times hardcover nonfiction list dated 13 September 2026, at number 15, in its 27th week.

## The short answer

This is narrative history, not an economics text and not an investing manual. Sorkin follows a small cast of powerful men from early 1929 to the middle of 1933 and shows how borrowed money, secrecy and overconfidence fed a boom that could not last. Readers who want to feel how a mania looks from the inside, and who enjoy finance told as a human story, are well served. Readers who want a clear account of why a falling stock market turned into the Great Depression will find that part thin, and several reviewers have said so. It is also long, and the ordinary people who paid the highest price stay mostly offstage.

## Who it is for

- Investors who have only known rising markets and want a vivid picture of what the other side can look like.
- Readers who liked finance books built around personalities and want the same treatment for an older crisis.
- People working in banking, markets or regulation who want to know where rules such as the separation of banking activities came from.
- History readers who are comfortable with a large cast and a day-by-day pace.

## Who it is not for

- Readers looking for practical guidance on what to do with their own money.
- Students who need the monetary and policy explanation of the Depression set out step by step.
- Anyone who wants the story of the unemployed, the farmers and the small savers, who are not the focus here.
- Readers who prefer a short book: the publisher lists 592 pages.

## How the book is organised

The publisher lists the hardcover at 592 pages; the New York Review of Books gives 567 pages for the copy it reviewed, and an audiobook read by the author runs to about thirteen and a half hours. According to the book's Wikipedia entry, the story is told in date order and is built around selected days across roughly four years. It opens on 1 February 1929, some seven months before the market peaked, and its last chapter is set on 21 June 1933, when the Depression was at its midpoint and share prices had begun to recover.

The same entry says the book carries about a hundred pages of notes and references, an index, and a list of the main figures and the firms they were attached to, under the heading "The Cast of Characters and the Companies They Kept". Sorkin is reported to have worked on it for eight years. No chapter list was available from the publisher's page, so this review does not describe individual chapters. It is written from the publisher's details and the published reviews listed under Sources.

## Key ideas

### Borrowed money made the boom, and the bust

The reviews agree that credit sits at the centre of the story. In the late 1920s an investor could buy shares "on margin", which means putting down part of the price and borrowing the rest from a broker, who in turn borrowed from banks. The Wikipedia summary of the book says the deposit could be as low as ten percent.

*An illustration with round numbers, not taken from the book:* an investor buys shares worth 1,000 with 100 of savings and 900 of borrowed money. If the shares rise 10% to 1,100, the investor's own stake doubles from 100 to 200, before interest. If the shares fall 10% to 900, the stake is gone completely, and the lender wants its 900 back. Small moves in price become very large moves in wealth, in both directions. That is why falling prices forced selling, and selling forced prices down further.

### Stock pools: legal then, unthinkable now

A second theme is how tilted the market was. Wealthy operators formed "stock pools": groups that quietly bought up a company's shares, traded among themselves to push the price higher, let word reach the newspapers, and then sold to the smaller buyers who had been drawn in. Kirkus notes that the practice was lawful at the time. The book uses figures such as William Durant, the founder of General Motors, to show it at work. For a modern reader the lesson is less about one trick and more about how little the ordinary buyer could see.

### The bankers had every reason to stay optimistic

Two bankers carry much of the narrative: Charles Mitchell, who ran National City Bank, and Thomas Lamont of J.P. Morgan. The New York Review of Books describes how the book opens with Mitchell trying to complete a takeover of the Corn Exchange Bank that was to be paid for largely in his own bank's shares. When the market slid in October 1929, the deal depended on that share price holding, and he bought shares to prop it up. Mitchell was later questioned at the Senate's Pecora hearings in 1933 about selling shares to his wife to reduce his tax bill; he was acquitted of tax fraud but had to resign. Sorkin's point, as reviewers read it, is that men in this position could not afford to imagine a bad outcome. Kirkus adds that he wants Mitchell judged with more nuance than history has allowed.

### A few people saw it, and it did not save them

Jesse Livermore, a celebrated speculator, bet on prices falling and, according to the reviews, made about 100 million dollars in the crash. He then lost nearly all of it in later years. The book also follows well-known names who lost heavily, among them Groucho Marx and Winston Churchill. Read together, these stories undercut the comforting idea that being right once about the market amounts to a durable skill.

### Washington versus Wall Street

The publisher frames the book partly as a contest between the financial world and the government. President Hoover worried about speculation and pressed the Federal Reserve, the American central bank, to tighten credit; the Fed hesitated for fear of setting off a panic. After the crash, Senator Carter Glass became the name attached to the 1933 law that split ordinary deposit-taking banking from the business of selling securities. Kirkus reports that Sorkin shows Glass accepting important parts of his own bill only reluctantly, which is a useful reminder that reform is rarely as tidy as the textbook version.

## What works

- **It makes a distant event concrete.** Building the story around named people and specific days gives a reader something to hold on to that a chart of share prices cannot.
- **The research is deep.** Eight years of work and roughly a hundred pages of notes mean the scenes rest on letters, archives and newspapers, not on guesswork.
- **It explains the machinery in passing.** Margin loans and stock pools are shown in use, which is an easier way to learn them than a definition.
- **It resists simple villains.** Treating Mitchell and Glass as complicated figures is more honest than a morality tale, even if a reader ends up disagreeing.
- **It is relevant without straining.** The pattern of easy credit, confident insiders and ignored warnings needs no forced modern parallel to make its point.

## What to be aware of

- **The economics is the weak side.** The New York Review of Books argues that the focus on individuals crowds out analysis of why the crash happened and why the official response failed. Balanced budgets, tariffs, the gold standard and timid central banks get less attention than the personalities.
- **Monetary policy is underplayed, on one reading.** The Wikipedia entry records a critique published by the Cato Institute which praises the research and faults the book for neglecting the Federal Reserve and the shrinking money supply.
- **It is a view from the top.** Kirkus observes that the millions who lost jobs, homes and savings are seldom heard from. Sorkin says so at the start, so it is a choice and not an oversight, but it limits what the book can tell you about the Depression itself.
- **The dead cannot be interviewed.** Sorkin's earlier book drew on conversations with living participants. Here the scenes are rebuilt from documents, and the New York Review of Books found the result only works some of the time.
- **It offers no method.** A reader hoping for rules to protect a portfolio will not find them, and should be wary of drawing confident forecasts from one episode.
- **The author is a prominent financial journalist and television host.** That brings access and fluency; it also means the book is written by someone close to the world it describes.

## How it compares

The obvious comparison is John Kenneth Galbraith's The Great Crash 1929, first published in 1955. The New York Review of Books calls it much shorter and credits it with covering both the human story and the causes. Liaquat Ahamed's Lords of Finance (2009) is the book the same review points to for the central bankers and the gold standard. A reader who wants the why should take one of those; a reader who wants the who and the day-by-day should take Sorkin. His own Too Big to Fail (2009) applies the same method to the 2008 crisis.

On this site, [A Random Walk Down Wall Street](../2026-09-28-review-a-random-walk-down-wall-street/) treats bubbles as a recurring feature of markets and then tells the reader what to do about it, which 1929 does not. [The Psychology of Money](../2026-09-14-review-the-psychology-of-money/) covers the behaviour on display here, such as overconfidence and the pull of the crowd, in short general essays. [The Intelligent Investor](../2026-10-05-review-the-intelligent-investor/) is the place to go for the difference between investing and speculating.

## The verdict

1929 is best read as a long, well-researched story about how intelligent, well-placed people talked themselves into disaster. As an explanation of the Great Depression it is incomplete, and it should be paired with a shorter analytical history. As a corrective for anyone who assumes markets only go one way, it does its job.

## FAQ

### Does the book explain what caused the Great Depression?

Only in part. It concentrates on the crash and the people around it. Reviewers in the New York Review of Books and elsewhere say the wider causes, such as monetary policy and the gold standard, are treated lightly.

### Do I need a finance background to follow it?

No. It is written for general readers, and terms such as margin and stock pools are shown through events. Some patience with a large cast of bankers helps.

### How long is it?

The publisher lists the hardcover at 592 pages, a good share of which is notes and index. The audiobook runs to about thirteen and a half hours.

### Did it make the New York Times list?

Yes. It was number 15 on the hardcover nonfiction list dated 13 September 2026, its 27th week on that list.
