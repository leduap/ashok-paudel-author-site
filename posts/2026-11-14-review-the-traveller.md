The Traveller is Andrea Wulf's biography of George Forster, the eighteenth-century naturalist who sailed with Captain Cook and later helped found a short-lived German republic. The edition reviewed here is the Allen Lane hardback of 2 June 2026, listed at 512 pages. In the United States the same life appeared a week later from Knopf, retitled The Traveler, with a subtitle that runs from the Pacific to revolutionary Paris. Wulf's own site also lists a German edition, Der Reisende, from C. Bertelsmann on 30 September 2026. Her site calls the English book shortlisted for the 2026 Baillie Gifford Prize.

## The short answer

A chronological life of a man who packed several careers into thirty-nine years: child collector, teenage draughtsman on a naval voyage, writer, professor, and revolutionary who died in exile. Wulf is at her best, reviewers agree, when Forster is moving, in Russia and on the Resolution. She gives less room to his political thinking, and a History Today review argues that she lets his principles shine more brightly than his prejudices. Read it as the best general introduction in English to a figure specialists already argue about. Do not read it as the last word on the Enlightenment, or as a book in which Forster is always the most enlightened person in the room.

This review can describe the shape of the life from long reviews and from Wulf's own pages. A clean publisher's table of contents was not opened. An online contents list for the US edition was too broken to trust as chapter titles, so those titles are not invented here.

## Who it is for

- Readers who like a life told in order, from childhood to a deathbed, with the places doing some of the work.
- Readers who know Cook's voyages and have never heard the name of the young man who wrote one of the sharpest accounts of the second one.
- Anyone interested in how an eighteenth-century European could argue against slavery and still repeat the insults of his time.
- Readers who want the personal story, including a painful marriage, and not only the ideas.

## Who it is not for

- Readers who want a history of the French Revolution, with Mainz as a chapter inside it. Mainz is here, but not at the length its importance might demand.
- Specialists who already work on Forster in German. History Today says the English books until now have mostly been for them, and that this one is the general reader's book.
- Anyone who wants a moral hero with no ugly sentences left in the record.
- Readers who dislike biography that reconstructs scenes closely from letters and journals. That is Wulf's method, and the Guardian both praises it and shows how far it goes.

## How the book is organised

The book follows the life. History Today and a long essay by Jenny Uglow in the New York Review of Books describe the same sequence, which is what this section relies on.

Childhood and the Russian journey come first, then the three years on the Resolution, then the book of the voyage, the European posts and the marriage, and finally Mainz and the months in Paris. The dates and the family detail are in the sections below. What matters for the shape is that the book is one timeline, and that the voyage is allowed to take a large share of it.

Uglow says the US edition has maps, illustrations, notes and a bibliography. A quotation carried on Wulf's own site, from the New Statesman, puts the endnotes at more than a hundred dense pages. The main text is kept moving. The Knopf page at Penguin Random House lists 512 pages, the same figure as Allen Lane. Uglow's review lists the Knopf book at 484 pages and $38. The two counts are both in print in sources opened for this review. They have not been reconciled by looking at a physical copy.

## Key ideas

### The journey is the argument

The Guardian says the book's real question is how Forster came to reject racial hierarchies that were normal around him, in public opinion and in philosophers including Kant. The title is the answer Wulf offers. Travel is not a colourful opening. It is the thing she thinks changed his mind. That is why, the Guardian notes, an unusual share of a whole life is given to three years at sea. History Today agrees that the Russian journey and the Cook voyage are where the writing is most alive, "almost novelistic". If you want the ideas without the ice and the islands, this is the wrong balance, and Wulf has chosen it on purpose.

### What he thought he saw in the Pacific

Uglow's essay, which tracks the book closely, describes Forster learning names, tattoos, canoes and dances, and noticing that people from distant islands could understand each other after a little adjustment. From that, and from the breadfruit, which had to be carried by people because the cultivated fruit had no seeds, he argued that Pacific peoples had a shared origin and had come from the west. Uglow says later genetics and archaeology backed the migration claim. She also quotes his gloom about the voyages themselves. Contact, as he wrote it, had so far done the islanders harm, and the communities hurt least were the ones that kept away. History Today adds that the 1777 book attacked two habits at once: the picture of Pacific societies as simple and unchanging, and the European habit of condemning cannibalism while waging wars of conquest.

The same essay is clear that he was not a sentimentalist. He recorded cruelty, including cruelty to women, and he used words such as "savage". Uglow's reading is that he used that word for "foreign", and that he refused the next step, which was to rank those foreigners as lesser. A reader should treat that as the reviewer's interpretation of his usage.

### He fought the race theorists, and he was not clean

History Today is the bluntest of the reviews opened here. Forster took on Kant's division of humanity into races, and then Christoph Meiners, who defended the slave trade. He opposed slavery, dispossession and exploitation. The same review says Wulf does not stay long enough with the prejudices Forster still repeated. It quotes him calling Poles "draught animals in human form", and likening some racial differences to the difference between donkeys and horses. The interesting man, on this account, is not a modern liberal born early. He is someone who could hold an emancipatory principle and a stereotype in the same head. Wulf, the review says, is more drawn to the principle.

### A father, a marriage, and no peace at home

Both Uglow and History Today say the private life is given real space, and that this is unusual next to specialist studies, which have preferred the ideas. Reinhold used his eldest son as a worker, sold the boy's drawings, and followed him with demands for years. The marriage to Therese Heyne, in 1785, is described as a disaster he partly agreed to live inside, including her affairs. They had daughters. Uglow's account includes a cancelled expedition, a second lover, and Therese leaving with the children while he stayed with the Revolution. History Today calls his acceptance of the affairs agonised, and thinks the personal detail is worth having. It also thinks those pages were taken from the politics.

### Mainz, and a revolution he would not leave

When the Bastille fell, Uglow writes, Forster was jubilant, and he did not turn away when former friends did. He became a leading figure in the Mainz republic, travelled to Paris to ask that it join France, and was left with a reputation in Germany as a traitor. In Paris he found the Terror, not the harmony he had wanted. Uglow quotes his disgust once he decided the Revolution had no virtue in it, and describes him dying in a garret in January 1794, still writing. History Today's criticism is structural. Fifteen months separate the French occupation of Mainz from his death, and those months, the review says, get only a few dozen pages. The Enlightenment around him is painted broadly enough to hide how many Enlightenments there were. The adventure is the book's success. The political thought is the book's shortfall.

## What works

- **The life is actually one story.** Forster's years do not naturally cohere. History Today calls the joining of them a feat in itself.
- **The voyage is allowed to take the time it took.** Three years at sea are not rushed into a prelude.
- **She uses letters that English readers have barely had.** The publisher and Wulf's site both say the correspondence is mostly unpublished in English.
- **She went to the places.** Uglow says Wulf followed the trail in Europe and the South Pacific, and that the notes and maps are there for anyone who wants the scaffolding.
- **The marriage and the father are not tidied away.** A life of ideas that omitted them would be thinner, and History Today says so even while wanting more politics.

## What to be aware of

- **Forster is idealised.** History Today's case is specific: the prejudices are in the record, and the book does not reckon with them as fully as with the anti-slavery writing.
- **The politics are short.** The Mainz months are the crisis of the life and, on that review's count, a small part of the book. Readers who come for the Revolution will feel the tilt toward the Pacific.
- **The intellectual world is simplified.** A broad brush for "the Enlightenment" makes Forster stand out more cleanly, and it hides arguments among his contemporaries.
- **Other books exist.** History Today says there is already a run of Forster studies in German and in English, mostly for specialists. This one is the readable entry, not the only entry.
- **The editions do not even agree how long they are.** Allen Lane and the Knopf catalogue page both say 512 pages. Uglow says 484. The US subtitle is different. Pick the Allen Lane book if you want the title used here.
- **Scene-painting is a choice.** The Guardian admires the move from the archive into what Forster saw and felt. A reader who wants daylight between the letter and the reconstructed moment will sometimes want more daylight than Wulf leaves.

## How it compares

[Alexander: God, King, Man](../2026-11-13-review-alexander-god-king-man/) is the other new biography on this site, and it won the prize for which Wulf's book was shortlisted, on her own account of the shortlist. Both are fast lives of famous travellers, built to be read rather than consulted. Richardson has to keep saying that his best stories are centuries late. Wulf is in a better position: Forster wrote, and other people wrote to him. The limit on her book is sympathy, not silence in the sources. [1929](../2026-09-11-review-1929/) is the site's other character-led history. It too is stronger on people and mood than on the underlying argument, which is the same criticism History Today makes of the political chapters here.

Wulf's earlier The Invention of Nature, on Alexander von Humboldt, is the book that led her to Forster, Uglow says. It is not reviewed on this site. Readers who know it will recognise the method: a scientific life told as a journey, with the ideas carried by places.

## The verdict

The book to give someone who has never heard of George Forster and should have. It makes the voyages and the family unforgettable, and it leaves his political mind and his worse remarks thinner than they deserve. Read History Today's objections alongside it, not instead of it.

## FAQ

### Who was George Forster?

A naturalist and writer, born in 1754 near what is now Gdańsk, dead in Paris in 1794. At seventeen he sailed on Cook's second voyage. At the end of his life he was a leader of the Mainz republic and an exile. Wulf's case is that he mattered, and that English-language memory dropped him.

### Why are there two English titles?

Allen Lane published The Traveller, with the subtitle about his revolutionary life and his search for humanity, on 2 June 2026. Knopf published The Traveler, with a subtitle about the South Seas and revolutionary Paris, on 9 June 2026. Wulf's site lists both, and a German edition later in the year. This review is of the Allen Lane book.

### Is it only about the voyage?

No. The voyage is the largest share, and reviewers say that is deliberate. The later chapters cover his writing on race, his marriage, his academic posts and the Revolution. History Today thinks those later ideas needed more pages than they got.

### Did Wulf only work from libraries?

No. Uglow says she also retraced his routes in Europe and the South Pacific. The book still rests on his publications, letters and diaries, and on what his contemporaries wrote. The notes are long. One review quoted on her site puts them at more than a hundred pages.
