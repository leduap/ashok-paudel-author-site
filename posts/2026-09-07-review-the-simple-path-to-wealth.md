The Simple Path to Wealth is JL Collins's guide to investing for financial independence. The edition reviewed here was self-published in 2016, with a foreword by the blogger Mr. Money Mustache. Its library record describes it as drawn from the author's blog posts and from a series of letters about money written to his teenage daughter, and that origin shows: it reads like advice from someone who wants to save you trouble, not like a textbook.

## The short answer

A direct, readable argument for keeping investing simple: own the whole stock market through one broad, low-cost index fund, keep adding to it, and hold it through the falls. It is one of the clearest first investing books for readers in the United States, and its real subject is nerve as much as funds. Its weaknesses are that the practical detail is American, the examples lean on one fund company, and its confidence that "the market always goes up" rests on United States history. A revised edition came out in 2025, so check which edition you are buying.

## Who it is for

- Beginners who want one clear approach instead of a menu of options.
- Readers who are put off by financial jargon.
- People working towards financial independence who want the investing side explained.
- Investors who have sold in a panic before and want to understand why that was costly.

## Who it is not for

- Readers outside the United States who need guidance on their own accounts and tax rules.
- Readers who want to compare many strategies or analyse individual companies.
- Cautious investors who want a balanced mix of shares and bonds from the start. Collins argues against that for anyone still building wealth.
- Anyone who needs current figures: this edition dates from 2016.

## How the book is organised

The 2016 edition runs to 265 pages. Its library record lists five parts and an afterword: "Beginnings", "Orientation", "How to harness the world's most powerful wealth building tool", "Magic Beans" and "What to do when you get there".

The book grew out of the "stock series" on the author's blog, which is still free to read. I have checked the ideas below against that series, which sets out the same argument in the author's own words. Where a figure is quoted, it comes from the series, and the book's own tables may differ.

## Key ideas

### Crashes are certain, and they are not the danger

The series opens with a part titled "There's a Major Market Crash Coming!!!!" The point is that crashes are a normal part of owning shares. Collins lists the ones he has invested through, from the recession of 1974-75 to the crash of 1987, the technology crash and 2008, and tells his daughter to expect a meltdown on the scale of 2008 every 15 to 20 years over an investing life.

The danger, he argues, is the investor's reaction. He tells the story against himself: after the crash of 1987 he held on for three or four months, then lost his nerve and sold close to the bottom, and bought back in a year or so later at a higher price. "Everybody makes money when the market is rising," he writes; what decides the outcome is what you do while it is collapsing.

### Why he believes the market always goes up

This is the book's boldest claim, and Collins gives two reasons for it. The first is that a market index is "self cleansing". A failing company can lose at most 100% of its value, after which it drops out and is replaced; a successful one can grow by many times. An index that holds every company therefore leans upward over time. The second is that shares are not slips of paper but part-ownership of real businesses, each trying to grow.

He is careful to say this is not a smooth ride, and that it applies to the whole market held through an index fund, not to individual shares or to funds whose managers try to beat the system.

### One fund is enough

From there the recommendation follows. For someone building wealth, Collins suggests a single total stock market index fund. An index fund simply buys every company in a market index instead of paying a manager to choose among them, which is why it is cheap. His example throughout is Vanguard's total United States stock market fund.

He turns the usual saying around: put all your eggs in one basket and forget about it. He accepts that holding 100% shares is considered very aggressive and says that is the point for an investor with decades ahead. He also notes that an 80% shares and 20% bonds mix has done about as well in some studies with a little less movement, and has no quarrel with readers who prefer it.

### Two stages, three tools

Collins divides an investing life into a wealth building stage and a wealth preservation stage, and says the stage depends on your plans, not your age. He needs only three tools: the stock index fund for growth, a total bond market index fund to smooth the ride and to protect against deflation (falling prices), and cash for daily needs and emergencies. Moving towards preservation means adding bonds; that is the whole adjustment.

He dismisses the common rule of holding your age as a percentage in bonds, on the grounds that even a 60-year-old may be investing for another 30 years and inflation eats bonds.

### The 4% rule, read properly

On living off a portfolio, Collins explains the research behind the "4% rule": a 1998 study by three professors at Trinity University, which tested how different mixes of shares and bonds survived different withdrawal rates over 30-year periods. The best-known result is that a half shares, half bonds portfolio, with 4% of its starting value taken out each year and raised with inflation, survived 30 years in 96% of the periods tested.

His reading is more useful than the slogan. Four per cent is a guide, not a guarantee; the portfolios that survived best held more shares; and costs matter greatly, since the same research showed the success rate falling sharply once fees of 1% or 2% a year were added. What gives security, he says, is being willing to spend less in bad years.

*An illustration with round numbers, not taken from the book:* someone who spends 3,000 a month spends 36,000 a year. For 36,000 to be 4% of a portfolio, the portfolio must be 25 times as large, or 900,000.

### "F-You Money"

The blunt phrase is the author's name for savings large enough to let you walk away from a job. It does not have to be enough to retire on. Collins's argument is that the most valuable thing money buys is freedom, and that people who live from one pay cheque to the next, or who carry debt, have very little of it. This is the motive that runs under the whole book.

## What works

- **It makes one recommendation and explains it.** A new investor finishes knowing what the author would do and why.
- **It treats behaviour as the main risk.** The chapters on crashes prepare the reader for bad years instead of pretending they will not come.
- **The author admits his own mistakes,** which makes the advice easier to trust.
- **The voice is conversational.** Short chapters, plain words, no formulas.
- **It covers the whole journey,** from the first savings to taking money out.

## What to be aware of

- **"The market always goes up" is a statement about the past.** The evidence is the history of the United States stock market. Nothing guarantees the next decades will look the same, and Collins himself concedes that if the market one day fails to recover, nothing else will be safe either. A portfolio held entirely in shares can fall a long way and stay down for years.
- **It is written for the United States.** Account types, tax rules and fund names will not match what is available elsewhere.
- **It recommends one company.** Collins says plainly that Vanguard is the only investment company he recommends, because it is owned by its own funds. He states that he is not paid by it. The reasoning about low costs applies to any cheap, broad index fund, but the book reads at times like an advertisement.
- **The 100% shares advice asks a lot.** It works only for readers who really will not sell in a crash. Many people discover they are not that investor only when the crash arrives.
- **There is a newer edition.** The author announced a revised edition for May 2025 with updated figures, expanded content, a new case study and reorganised chapters, and calls it a better book than the original. The edition linked on this page is the 2016 original.

## How it compares

Against [The Little Book of Common Sense Investing](../2026-09-21-review-the-little-book-of-common-sense-investing/), this is the warmer and more personal book, and it goes further into what to do at each stage of life. Bogle's book is stronger on the arithmetic of costs and more cautious about holding bonds.

[A Random Walk Down Wall Street](../2026-09-28-review-a-random-walk-down-wall-street/) reaches the same conclusion with far more evidence and history in a much longer book. [The Psychology of Money](../2026-09-14-review-the-psychology-of-money/) covers the behaviour side in more depth but gives no instructions. For the spending side of financial independence, [Your Money or Your Life](../2026-10-12-review-your-money-or-your-life/) is the fuller treatment.

## The verdict

A good first book on index investing for an American reader, and the clearest statement of the keep-it-simple school. Its best pages are about staying put when markets fall. Readers elsewhere can take the reasoning from it but will need another source for the practical steps, and cautious readers should treat the all-shares advice as one view, not a rule.

## FAQ

### Which edition does this review cover?

The 2016 first edition, a paperback of 265 pages according to its Open Library record. The author published a revised and expanded edition in 2025.

### Is it useful outside the United States?

The reasoning about costs, diversification and patience applies anywhere. The chapters on accounts and tax do not, so you would need a local guide for those.

### Do I need to know anything about investing first?

No. It is written for readers starting from nothing.

### Can I read the same material for free?

Much of it. The stock series the book grew out of is on the author's website. The book is edited and arranged in order.
