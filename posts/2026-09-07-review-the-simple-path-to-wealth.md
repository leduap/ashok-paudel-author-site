The Simple Path to Wealth is JL Collins's guide to investing for financial independence. The edition reviewed here was self-published in 2016, with a foreword by the blogger Mr. Money Mustache. Its library record describes it as drawn from the author's blog posts and from a series of letters about money written to his teenage daughter, and that origin shows: it reads like advice from someone who wants to save you trouble.

## The short answer

A direct, readable argument for keeping investing simple: own the stock market through one broad, low-cost index fund and hold it for a long time, including through the falls. It works well as a first investing book for readers in the United States. The detail on accounts and tax is American, and a revised edition came out in 2025, so check which edition you are buying.

## Who it is for

- Beginners who want one clear approach instead of a menu of options.
- Readers who are put off by financial jargon.
- People working towards financial independence who want the investing side explained.

## Who it is not for

- Readers outside the United States who need guidance on their own accounts and tax rules.
- Readers who want to compare many strategies or analyse individual companies.
- Anyone who needs current figures: this edition dates from 2016.

## Key ideas

The book grew out of the "stock series" on the author's blog, and the topics of that series give a fair picture of the ground it covers:

- **One broad index fund as the core holding.** The series uses a Vanguard total stock market index fund as its main example, and includes a part on what to do if you cannot buy that fund.
- **Market falls are part of the deal.** The series opens with a part on market crashes and follows it with one titled "The Market Always Goes Up". The message is to expect large falls and not to sell because of them.
- **Bonds and living off a portfolio.** Later parts cover bonds, the "4% rule" and withdrawal rates, and target retirement funds as an even simpler choice.
- **"F-You Money".** The author's blunt name for savings large enough to give you choices about how you work.
- **United States account types.** There are parts on health savings accounts and on required minimum distributions from tax-deferred accounts.

The book itself is arranged in parts that run from "Beginnings" and "Orientation" through to "What to do when you get there".

## What works

- **It makes one recommendation and explains it.** A new investor finishes knowing what the author would do and why.
- **The voice is conversational.** Short chapters, plain words, no formulas.
- **It prepares the reader for bad years** instead of pretending they will not come.

## What to be aware of

- **It is written for the United States.** Account types, tax rules and fund names will not match what is available elsewhere.
- **The examples lean on one fund company.** The idea of a broad, low-cost index fund travels; the specific funds may not.
- **The case rests on history.** Past stock market returns do not guarantee future ones, and a portfolio held mostly in shares can fall a long way and stay down for years.
- **There is a newer edition.** The author announced a revised edition for May 2025, with updated figures, added material and a new section answering reader questions. The edition linked on this page is the 2016 original.

## The verdict

A good first book on index investing for an American reader, and a clear statement of the keep-it-simple school. Readers elsewhere can take the reasoning from it but will need another source for the practical steps. For the arithmetic behind the same idea, see the review of [The Little Book of Common Sense Investing](../2026-09-21-review-the-little-book-of-common-sense-investing/); for the academic background, [A Random Walk Down Wall Street](../2026-09-28-review-a-random-walk-down-wall-street/).

## FAQ

### Which edition does this review cover?

The 2016 first edition, a paperback of 265 pages according to its Open Library record. The author published a revised and expanded edition in 2025.

### Is it useful outside the United States?

The reasoning about costs, diversification and patience applies anywhere. The chapters on accounts and tax do not, so you would need a local guide for those.

### Do I need to know anything about investing first?

No. It is written for readers starting from nothing.
