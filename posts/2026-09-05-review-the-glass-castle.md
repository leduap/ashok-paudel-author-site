The Glass Castle is Jeannette Walls's memoir of growing up in a family that was almost always broke and frequently on the run from its debts. Scribner first published it in March 2005. More than twenty years later the paperback was number 13 on the New York Times combined print and e-book nonfiction list dated 6 September 2026, its 67th week on that list.

## The short answer

This is a memoir, not a finance book, and it should be read as one. Its subject is a childhood shaped by two gifted, unreliable parents, and the slow decision by their children to leave. It earns a place here because few books show so concretely what unstable money does inside a household: the midnight departures, the hunger, the hopes pinned on a scheme that never arrives. It also contains one of the more memorable accounts of saving towards a goal under the worst conditions. Walls does not lecture and does not condemn, which is the book's strength and, for a reader wanting conclusions, its limit. Some of the content is disturbing.

## Who it is for

- Readers who want to understand poverty from the inside, without statistics or argument.
- Anyone who grew up with money trouble at home and wants to see it described honestly.
- Readers interested in how children form their own habits around money in reaction to their parents.
- Book groups: the parents divide opinion, and that makes for discussion.

## Who it is not for

- Readers looking for guidance on budgeting, debt or investing. There is none.
- Anyone who wants the author to pass judgement on her parents. She largely declines to.
- Readers who would be upset by accounts of child neglect, hunger and sexual abuse.
- People who want analysis of the causes of poverty. The book is one family's story.

## How the book is organised

The Open Library record for the Scribner paperback lists 288 pages, and the New York Times gave the same figure for the 2005 hardcover. According to the book's Wikipedia entry it is in five parts.

Part One, "A Woman on the Street", is a short opening set in New York, where the adult author sees her mother searching through rubbish. Part Two, "The Desert", covers early childhood in the American Southwest, with the family moving every few months. Part Three, "Welch", is the longest stretch of time, about seven years in a declining coal town in West Virginia where her father grew up. Part Four, "New York City", follows the children's departure one by one and then, unexpectedly, the parents' arrival. Part Five, "Thanksgiving", is a brief closing scene some years after the father's death.

The shape is a frame around a straight chronological account. This review is written from the published reviews, summaries and catalogue details listed under Sources.

## Key ideas

### Charm and neglect in the same people

The parents are the heart of the book. The New York Times review describes Rex Walls as talented and magnetic, able to talk his way into work as a mining engineer or electrician, and unable to keep any job because of his drinking. Rose Mary Walls is a painter who held that children benefit from being left to cope. Both distrusted institutions and called their way of living self-reliance. Kirkus notes that the children were largely left to look after themselves and were even taught to shoplift. Walls shows what was attractive about these people as clearly as what was harmful, and reviewers consistently praise that balance.

### Life with no margin

The family's term for leaving town in a hurry, usually at night and ahead of creditors, was "doing the skedaddle". The summaries describe what followed from it: broken schooling, houses without heating or plumbing, days without food. An early episode sets the pattern. At three the author was badly burned while cooking for herself, and her father removed her from hospital without settling the account. Later the children collected cans and bottles to sell so that they could eat. For a reader used to thinking about money in terms of plans, the book is an education in what the absence of any buffer looks like. Every setback becomes an emergency, and every emergency is met by moving on.

### The castle that was never built

The title refers to the father's promise to build a glass house in the desert, to be paid for by an invention for finding gold or by his ideas on energy. He drew detailed plans. The LitCharts summary records the moment the promise collapsed for the author: she and her brother dug a foundation pit behind the house in Welch, and their father had them fill it with rubbish because the family could not afford the collection fee. The story is a sharp picture of a familiar financial pattern, in which a large future windfall is used to excuse the neglect of small present obligations.

### An escape fund

The most practical thread is the plan made by the two eldest sisters to get to New York. They saved for it. Their father took the money and spent it on drink and gambling. They started again. The older sister left after finishing school and worked in a restaurant; the author followed after her eleventh year of school, found work at a newspaper, and went on to Barnard College. One by one the younger children were brought out too. There is no formula here, but the elements are recognisable to anyone who has read about saving: a specific goal, a plan shared between two people, and persistence after a loss.

### What ownership means afterwards

The book opens with the author living at an expensive Manhattan address and closes at a country house she owns. The LitCharts analysis draws attention to the tension this creates. Her mother regards the wish for comfort as a confusion of values, and the parents, once in New York, live as squatters by choice. Walls does not resolve the argument. She shows that possessions gave her stability she had lacked, and also that lack of possessions was not the family's only problem.

## What works

- **It shows without preaching.** Events are set down plainly and the reader is left to react, which makes them harder to dismiss.
- **It is fair to difficult people.** Kirkus observes that Walls neither demonises nor idealises her parents, and the book is more convincing for it.
- **The detail is concrete.** A pit filled with rubbish and a raided escape fund teach more about scarcity than a page of description.
- **The children's resourcefulness is credible.** The New York Times reviewer singles out their intelligence and resilience, and the escape is earned step by step.
- **It has lasted.** Wikipedia records a stay of over seven years on the New York Times list and translation into 31 languages.

## What to be aware of

- **It is a memoir, with a memoir's limits.** The account rests on one person's recollection of events from early childhood onwards. Nothing in the sources consulted corroborates or disputes particular episodes.
- **It can be read as a bootstraps story.** Kirkus calls it exactly that. A reader should be careful about concluding that determination is all a poor child needs, since these children also had a well-educated mother, a father described as brilliant, and siblings who helped one another.
- **It offers no way out for anyone else.** The lessons about money are implied. Nothing is set out as advice, and the circumstances are extreme.
- **Some content is distressing.** Wikipedia notes that the book has been challenged in some American schools for its language and sexual content, and it describes abuse and hunger involving children.
- **The author withholds judgement.** Readers who want the parents held to account may find the even tone frustrating.

## How it compares

On this site the closest companion is the review of [Born a Crime](../2026-09-02-review-born-a-crime/), another memoir of a poor childhood. That book explains poverty partly through a political system; this one locates it inside a single family's choices and troubles. Together they make the point that being short of money has more than one cause.

[The Psychology of Money](../2026-09-14-review-the-psychology-of-money/) argues that early experience fixes people's attitudes to risk and security. The adult author's attachment to a settled home is a clear instance. [Your Money or Your Life](../2026-10-12-review-your-money-or-your-life/) asks readers to work out what is enough; the argument between Walls and her mother over comfort is the same question in dramatic form.

Wikipedia also records a 2017 film adaptation, which received mixed reviews. The Scribner paperback is the edition on the current list.

## The verdict

A direct, unsentimental memoir that has kept its readers for two decades. It will not tell anyone what to do with money. It will show, more vividly than most finance books manage, what happens to a household that has none and no plan for getting any, and what it took for four children to build something steadier.

## FAQ

### Is The Glass Castle a true story?

It is published as a memoir, the author's own account of her life. As with any memoir, it reflects her memory and point of view.

### Why is it called The Glass Castle?

The title comes from a house of glass her father promised to build for the family in the desert. It was never built.

### Is it suitable for teenagers?

It is taught in some American high schools and has also been challenged there because of its language and its references to abuse. An adult may wish to read it first.

### How long is it?

The paperback is listed at 288 pages, in five parts.
