Anthropic filed an amended prospectus on Nov. 2 and could start trading on Nasdaq within weeks. Few American savers will get shares in the offering. Many of them will own the stock anyway, and soon. They won't choose to buy it, and they won't get any say in the price.

That is the work of a rule most savers have never heard of. This year Nasdaq opened a "fast entry" lane for its flagship Nasdaq-100 index. A new listing whose market value ranks among the index's 40 largest companies can now join after 15 trading days instead of serving the usual seasoning of at least three full calendar months. In May, FTSE Russell, whose indexes benchmark some $20 trillion by the company's count, adopted its own version: qualifying IPOs are added after their fifth trading day. Once a stock is in the index, the funds that track it have to buy it. They can't haggle, and they can't wait.

The first test came this summer. SpaceX listed on June 12 and joined the Nasdaq-100 on July 7. JPMorgan estimated that index funds would need to buy about $4.3 billion of the stock, and tracking funds generally buy at the closing price on the day before a change takes effect. On July 6 that price was $160.42. Six sessions later the stock closed at $136.08, down 15%. On Nov. 3 it closed at $141.31, still about 12% below what the index funds paid. Traders who bought ahead of the index funds found a guaranteed buyer when they wanted to sell.

At any valuation reported so far, Anthropic would rank comfortably among the index's 40 largest companies. Revenue for the first nine months of 2026 was $33.2 billion, about 13 times the figure a year earlier. But the company has also taken on about $895 billion of commitments, almost all of it for computing and infrastructure, with $69.8 billion due over the next 12 months. The number of shares on offer is still blank. A huge valuation spread over a thin float is exactly what a buyer who ignores price shouldn't be chasing.

None of this is an argument against the IPO. It is good news when a company this size picks the public market, where ordinary investors can own it, over staying private, where only institutions and the wealthy can. What matters is who arrives first. Discovering the price is the job of active investors, who read the prospectus, argue over margins and risk their own money. Index money should come in after that work is done, not halfway through it.

S&P Dow Jones Indices understood this. After a market consultation, it announced on June 4 that the S&P 500's rules would stay as they are. A new listing needs 12 months of trading and has to pass the profitability test before it can be considered, and size alone won't earn an exception. The money tracking the S&P 500 will therefore buy Anthropic, if it ever does, only after a year of public prices and several quarterly reports.

The index providers' case for speed deserves a hearing. An index that leaves out a giant for a year tracks the market less faithfully, and if the newcomer soars, its investors miss the gain. Traders can also front-run an addition at 12 months just as easily as at 15 days. All true. But a tracking gap is temporary and bounded, and it closes when the stock joins. Buying the thinnest float at the newest price, all on the same day, is concentrated risk taken on behalf of people who never chose it.

I work in corporate finance. When a company buys a business, the accounting rules put its actual results in the buyer's books from day one. Its forecast is another matter: it doesn't carry full weight in the group plan until a few quarters of closed books show the pitch-deck numbers were real. That isn't distrust; it keeps one optimistic number from steering everyone else's decisions. Seasoning does the same job for an index.

Savers are already heavily exposed. By Morningstar's count, five giants made up about 30% of the S&P 500 in September, and Morgan Stanley puts AI-related stocks near 45% of the index. Fast entry adds the riskiest piece: a newly listed company at its newest price, bought when the fewest of its shares trade.

The fix doesn't require new regulation. Index providers compete for licensing fees, and fund sponsors pick their own benchmarks. Retirement-plan committees, which owe a fiduciary duty to participants, can put one question to every fund on their menu: does its index buy giant IPOs within weeks of listing? If the answer is yes, they can choose a seasoned alternative or ask the provider to build one. Fund companies could also show on every statement how much of a saver's money sits in the fund's ten largest holdings and how much in stocks added during the past year. Investors who want early exposure to AI listings can still buy them, on purpose.

The AI boom may well justify its prices. If it does, savers will share in the gains whether their index adds Anthropic in three weeks or in a year. If it doesn't, the gap between those two dates is the gap between buying from the enthusiasts and serving as their exit. Passive money should be patient money. Let the market set the price first.
