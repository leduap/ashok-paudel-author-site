Every finance department I have known has a report like this. It arrives on the third working day of each month: dozens of pages of carefully reconciled tables, sent to a distribution list that has grown for a decade. A capable analyst spends a couple of days building it. Ask who actually reads it and you get a shrug. Nobody decided the report should exist for ever. Nobody ever decided it should stop.

That report is about to become immortal.

The government says that fully digitising public services could unlock more than £45bn a year in savings and productivity, most of it by taking manual work out of public servants' days, and its digital blueprint tells Whitehall's AI incubator to go after that figure. The workings it later shared with MPs assumed that every routine task, and a tenth of non-routine ones, could be automated. It is a bold number and I hope much of it proves real. But it skips a prior question: how much of that routine work should exist at all?

Here is what worries me, as someone who has spent a career in corporate controlling. Until now, pointless work had one reliable enemy: its cost. A report that ate two days of an analyst's month had to defend itself, at least occasionally, when budgets got tight. Cost was a crude deletion device, but it worked. AI removes it. When a monthly pack, a reconciliation or a compliance return can be generated in seconds, nobody will ever win the argument to kill it, because there is no longer a saving to point to.

The cost does not vanish. It moves. Producing information becomes almost free; reading it, checking it and deciding what to do about it does not. Microsoft's own workplace data already shows the average office worker receiving 117 emails a day, most of them skimmed in under a minute. Give every team a machine that writes fluent summaries on demand and the flood rises. In accounting terms this is a misallocated cost: caused in one department, carried by another, and visible on neither ledger. The producer's budget looks leaner. The reader's day gets worse. No dashboard records the difference.

I learned how much inherited work organisations carry when I moved all our entities on to a single system. The software was not the hard part. The hard part, and the most valuable, was the run of meetings in which every local report, approval step and side spreadsheet had to argue for its place in the new world. A surprising number could not. A migration forces the question "would we build this today?" An AI rollout lets you skip it, because automating yesterday's process is so much easier than redesigning it.

Accountants have had a tool for this since 1970, when Peter Pyhrr introduced zero-based budgeting at Texas Instruments: instead of rolling last year's spending forward, every pound must justify itself from scratch. We apply that discipline, unevenly, to money. We almost never apply it to work.

The arrival of AI is the moment to start, and it does not need a grand programme. Before any process is automated, someone should have to put their name to it and say who uses the output and which decision it changes. Recurring reports, approvals and internal returns should carry an expiry date and lapse unless an owner renews them. Most modern systems already log who opens what; that evidence belongs at the start of an automation project, not in the post-mortem. And success should be counted not only in hours saved by automation, but in work that no longer needs doing at all.

This matters most in the public sector, because much of its paperwork is not internal. It lands on citizens: forms, evidence requests, the same question asked by three different offices. Automating the state's side of a needless form makes it cheaper for government but no lighter for the carer, the small-business owner or the claimant filling it in. The productivity a country actually feels comes from removing steps, not from speeding them up.

I am not arguing against the technology. My teams use it, and at work worth doing it is genuinely impressive. I am arguing about sequence: subtract first, then automate. The organisations that gain most from AI will not be the ones that automate the most tasks, but the ones confident enough to switch some of them off.

There is a human dividend, too. The most demoralising part of many early-career jobs is producing things nobody reads. Clearing that away is how you give talented people time for judgment, for customers and for patients, the work that made them want the job in the first place.

The best AI project I can imagine begins with the delete key.
